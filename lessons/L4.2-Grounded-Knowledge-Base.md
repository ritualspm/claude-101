## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Grounded vs. guessing (the open-book exam)
Ask Claude a question with **no sources attached** and it answers from general knowledge — like a student sitting an exam from memory. Often right, sometimes confidently wrong, and you can't tell which without checking.

**Grounding** is handing that same student the **open book** and saying: *"answer only from these pages, and point to the line you used."* That's a **grounded knowledge base** — you give Claude your own material (PRDs, policies, experiment readouts, even a codebase) and it answers *from that*, not from the internet in its head.

The everyday PM version: instead of "what's our refund window, roughly?" you point Claude at your actual policy docs and ask the same thing. Now the answer is traceable to a real document you own.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A PM says: "I don't need to attach our policy docs — Claude already knows how delivery refunds usually work, so it'll just tell me ours." What's the best correction?*
- Fair enough — refund rules are pretty standard, so its general knowledge is close enough to rely on.
- It knows how refunds *generally* work, but not *your* specific policy — without your docs it's guessing, and you can't tell a guess from a fact. ✓
- True, but only if you're on Opus; Sonnet doesn't have that general knowledge.

### Concept 2 — Citations are the receipt
A grounded answer isn't just "the answer" — it comes with a **citation**: *which document, which lines.* That's the receipt. It turns a claim you'd have to trust into a claim you can **check in ten seconds** by opening the named doc.

Analogy: a good analyst doesn't just say "conversion dropped." They say "conversion dropped 0.3% — see row 14 of the readout." You can verify it. A citation does the same for Claude's answer. **No citation, no trust** — if it won't tell you where an answer came from, treat it as an unverified guess.

This is your safety habit for grounded work: read the answer, then glance at the source it cites and confirm the answer actually matches what's written there.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude gives you a crisp answer about your rider bonus scheme but names no source document. You're about to paste it into a leadership update. Best move?*
- Ask it which document and exact lines that came from, then open that doc and confirm before you use it. ✓
- Ship it — it sounds specific and detailed, and detail is a good sign it's right.
- Rephrase the question three different ways; if you get the same answer each time, it's true.

### Concept 3 — Grounded can still be wrong (faithfulness)
Here's the subtle one. Even *with* the right document attached, an answer can **drift** — it names the correct source but subtly misstates it, adds a number that isn't there, or blends two docs together. Whether the answer actually **matches the source it cites** is called **faithfulness**.

Analogy: a student cites the right page but paraphrases it wrong. The footnote is correct; the sentence isn't. That's an *unfaithful* answer — cited but inaccurate.

So the check has two halves: (1) did it cite the **right** source, and (2) does the answer **faithfully match** what that source actually says? Both must hold. This is exactly what an **eval** measures at scale — but you start by doing it by eye on one answer.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude cites the correct experiment readout, but its summary says conversion "rose 3%" when the doc says conversion was flat and not significant. How do you describe this?*
- The citation is right, so the answer is trustworthy — the number is a rounding detail.
- Grounding failed completely; the source it named must be the wrong one.
- It cited the right source but the answer isn't faithful to it — a citation alone isn't enough, the words have to match the doc. ✓

### Concept 4 — Scope your sources, keep them clean
A grounded answer is only as good as the shelf you point it at. Two habits:

- **Scope tightly.** Point Claude at the *specific* folder that holds the source of truth — not "everything." A tight, curated set gives cleaner, more traceable answers than a giant messy pile where three versions of the same doc disagree.
- **Keep it clean and safe.** Grounding uses *your* material, so the the rules still apply: **read-only** on the real docs, and **no customer PII or payment data (PCI)** in the source set. If a real doc contains personal data, use an anonymized copy for grounding. And this is read-and-answer work — Sonnet is the right default; you don't need Opus to look something up in your own docs.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want grounded answers about a launch. You have a clean folder of 6 final docs, and also a shared drive with 400 mixed files including old drafts and a spreadsheet of customer emails. What do you point Claude at?*
- The 400-file drive — more documents means a more complete, better-grounded answer.
- The clean 6-doc folder; the messy drive has contradicting drafts and customer PII you must keep out. ✓
- Both, but only on Opus so it's powerful enough to sort the mess itself.

## Part 2: Exercise — Ground an answer, then check the receipt

Fully self-contained. The demo docs already exist at **`sandboxes/rag/`** — four fake policy/PRD/experiment docs plus a tiny fake code snippet. Obviously fake, no real data. The learner does real hands-on grounding against them.

### Step 1 — Point Claude at the shelf (real action)
Have the learner ask Claude, in their own words, to **look only at the `sandboxes/rag/` folder** and list what's in it — one line each on what each document covers. Confirm they see roughly: a **refund policy**, a **rider incentive scheme**, a **checkout reorder PRD**, an **ETA padding experiment**, and a small **ETA code snippet**. Point out: this is the "open book" — the shelf Claude will answer *from*.

### Step 2 — Ask the grounded question (real action)
Have them paste the question from `sandboxes/rag/QUESTION.md`:

> "A rider completed 10 deliveries in a row on a rainy Saturday evening, all during peak hours. On top of the base pay, how much bonus did they earn, and which document says so? Quote the exact lines."

Tell them to insist on the citation and the exact quoted lines. A good grounded answer names **`rider-incentives.md`** and quotes the peak-hour bonus (€1.50/delivery on weekend evenings), the streak bonus (€6 for 10 in a row), and the rain guarantee (€0.80/delivery). Ask them, open-ended: **"Which document did it cite, and do the quoted lines actually say what the answer claims?"** (This is the faithfulness check by eye.)

### Step 3 — Contrast with an ungrounded guess (real action)
Now have them ask the *same* rider-bonus question in a **fresh Chat** (or plainly: "answer from general knowledge, no folder") — **no folder attached.** Ask them, open-ended: **"What's different about this answer? Does it name a source? Would you trust it in a leadership update?"** They should notice the ungrounded version invents plausible-but-sourceless numbers, while the grounded one points to a real line they can open and verify. That gap *is* the whole lesson.

### Optional stretch (live tools)
If they have Confluence or a Google Drive connected: point Claude at a **small, real, non-sensitive** doc set they own and ask a grounded question with citations. Same rules — **read-only**, **no PII/PCI** in the source set, verify the citation by opening the doc. Frame as optional; the demo folder is enough to pass.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You want to stand up a grounded knowledge base for one thing your squad answers the same question about over and over — refund rules, launch scope, an experiment decision. In 3–4 lines: (1) name the question and which docs you'd put on the shelf, (2) name one doc or data source you'd deliberately keep OUT and why, and (3) the one thing you'd check on every answer before trusting it."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (NEVER show it). The point isn't a perfect answer — it's that they can scope a clean source set, exclude the wrong stuff (contradicting drafts, PII/PCI), and name a verification habit (citation + faithfulness).

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Passing answer** covers the three asks with a real, specific example:
1. A concrete repeated question + a *scoped* set of source docs (not "all our docs").
2. Something deliberately kept out, with a reason — ideally contradicting drafts/old versions, OR customer PII/PCI, OR unrelated docs.
3. A verification habit before trusting an answer — at minimum "check the citation / open the source."

**Strong answer** additionally shows the L4.2 insight:
- Names **faithfulness explicitly** — not just "does it cite a source" but "does the answer actually match what the cited source says."
- Justifies the exclusion in trust terms (a messy pile produces contradictory, untraceable answers), not just tidiness.
- Reflects guardrails naturally: read-only on the real docs, anonymize/keep PII-PCI out of the source set, Sonnet is fine for look-up-in-your-own-docs work.
- Bonus: mentions this by-eye check is the manual version of an **eval** you'd run at scale.

**Needs revisit** if the answer treats a citation as proof on its own (misses faithfulness), points Claude at "everything," or would put sensitive/PII data on the shelf. Give one specific sharpen, then complete.
