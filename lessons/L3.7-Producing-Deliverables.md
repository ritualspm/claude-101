# L3.7 — Producing Deliverables (from messy notes to a thing you'd actually send)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 3 — Power Moves** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~9 min | Exercise ~9 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L3.6 — Working Agentically (give a goal + permissions, approve safely, let it run).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, likely brand new to Claude and possibly to the terminal. Teach through analogy and their own screen. Never show code. Anchor everything to real PM tasks — a leadership readout, a one-pager, a deck outline. By Level 3 they've orchestrated agents and personas; now they turn that horsepower into *finished outputs* other people read.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with a multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`) if available, else A/B/C. Correct option marked ✓ (never shown). One question per turn; always explain the outcome before the next concept.

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — A deliverable is a *shape*, not just words
Everything so far has produced answers in the chat. A **deliverable** is different: it's an output built to a **specific shape for a specific reader** — a one-pager for leadership, a deck outline for a kickoff, a visual for a Slack thread.

Think of it like ordering at a restaurant. "Something with chicken" gets you *something*. "A chicken sandwich, no mayo, cut in half, to go" gets you exactly what you can hand to someone. The shape is the order.

So the useful instinct isn't "summarize my notes" — it's naming the **artifact**:
- *"Turn this into a one-page leadership readout — problem, options, recommendation, ask."*
- *"Give me a 6-slide deck outline: one message per slide, speaker notes underneath."*
- *"Make me a simple visual of this funnel drop I can drop in Slack."*

Same raw material, three different shapes. You choose the shape by asking who reads it and what they need to *do* after reading.

**Check** (present the options in random order; ✓ = correct, never reveal it): *You paste six weeks of messy notes and type: "summarize this." You get a tidy paragraph — but it's not something you'd send to your VP. What was the real miss?*
- The notes were too messy — you need to clean them up yourself before Claude can do anything useful with them.
- You asked for a summary, not a **deliverable** — you never named the shape (one-pager, sections, audience) or who reads it. ✓
- You should have used Opus — summarizing six weeks of notes is a genuinely hard problem that Sonnet can't handle.

### Concept 2 — Feed it the raw pile; don't pre-polish
The reflex is to tidy your notes *before* handing them over. Resist it. Claude is at its best turning a **messy pile into structure** — that's the value. Pre-polishing wastes your time and, worse, strips out the raw signal (the offhand comment, the half-number, the disagreement) that makes the output honest.

It's like handing a tailor your actual measurements instead of guessing your size first. Give it everything — the contradictory quotes, the "idk the real number," the gut call — and let it organize.

Two habits ride along here:
- **Point at the file, don't paste a wall of text.** In Cowork, reference the notes file (or the folder) so Claude reads it directly. Cleaner, and nothing gets truncated.
- **guardrail — no real customer PII or payment data (PCI) in the pile.** If your genuine notes contain customer names, card data, or personal info, anonymize *first*. The messy-notes habit applies to *your* working notes, not raw operational records.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You've got a rambling notes file with contradictory quotes and a few "confirm this number later" placeholders. What's the smartest way to hand it to Claude for a one-pager?*
- Rewrite the notes into clean bullet points yourself first, then hand over the polished version so Claude has less to get wrong.
- Point Claude at the notes file as-is and ask for the one-pager — the messy pile is exactly what it's good at organizing. ✓
- Delete the "confirm later" placeholders before sending, since unconfirmed numbers shouldn't appear in a deliverable at all.

### Concept 3 — Iterate on the artifact, not just the text
The first draft is a *starting shape*, not the final thing. The power move is steering it like a real document: **"tighten section 2," "add a one-line risk callout," "cut it to half a page," "make the recommendation the headline."** You're editing an object, not re-asking a question.

Like working with a designer — you don't re-brief from scratch each round, you give targeted notes: "logo bigger, less text, move the CTA up." Same here.

And in **Cowork specifically**, a polished output can render as an **Artifact** — a real, viewable page (a formatted one-pager, a simple chart, a mock) you can look at, refine, and export, rather than scrolling raw text in the chat. That's the visual workspace doing what chat can't.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude hands you a solid one-pager draft, but the recommendation is buried in paragraph three and it's a touch long. What's the efficient next move?*
- Start a fresh task and re-paste the notes with a better prompt so you get a cleaner first draft this time.
- Accept it as-is — it's a first draft, and asking for changes tends to make Claude rewrite everything and lose the good parts.
- Give it targeted notes on the existing draft: "make the recommendation the headline, and cut it to half a page." ✓

### Concept 4 — A deliverable is a draft *for you*, never an auto-send
Producing a polished artifact makes it tempting to fire it straight into the channel or publish it to Confluence. Don't — and Claude won't. **Draft, never send.** The one-pager, the message, the Confluence page: all of it lands in *your* hands for a read-through and approval first.

This matters most on deliverables precisely *because* they look finished. A polished one-pager *feels* done — but you're still the one accountable for the numbers in it. Especially with placeholder figures ("confirm with analytics"), a human check before it leaves your machine isn't optional.

The rhythm: **Claude produces → you review and verify → you send.** The last step is always yours.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude produces a genuinely great one-pager for Thursday's leadership readout — formatted, on-message, ready-looking. A teammate says "just have it post to the leadership channel so it's done." Best response?*
- Fine to auto-post since it's clearly polished — the review step is really only needed for rough drafts.
- Keep it as a draft, read it through, and verify the numbers before *I* send it — it's a draft for me, and I'm accountable for what's in it. ✓
- Post it now and add a follow-up message later if anything turns out to be wrong.

## Part 2: Exercise — Shape a real deliverable from real mess

You'll take a genuinely messy notes file and turn it into a leadership one-pager. Everything here is **fake demo content** — safe to use freely.

The file: **`sandboxes/deliverables/raw-notes.md`** — six weeks of scrambled notes about a checkout completion drop at QuickMart DACH. Contradictory quotes, half-numbers, a gut call, "confirm later" placeholders. Exactly the pile you'd actually have before a review.

### Step 1 — Read the mess first (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open `sandboxes/deliverables/raw-notes.md` (or ask Claude to read it aloud in Cowork). Ask: **"Skim it. In your own words — what's the actual decision buried in here, and what's the one number leadership will care about most?"**
Validate: the decision is *roll back the address picker vs. hotfix vs. do nothing*; the number leadership will ask about is **revenue impact of the drop** (noted as "nobody has this yet"). Affirm if they spot that the notes are full of unconfirmed figures.

### Step 2 — Name the shape, then generate (hands-on)
Guide them to ask for a **deliverable, not a summary**. A good prompt names the artifact, the audience, and the sections — e.g.:
> *"Read `sandboxes/deliverables/raw-notes.md` and turn it into a one-page leadership readout for Thursday. Sections: Problem (with the numbers), Options (with tradeoffs), Recommendation, and The Ask. Flag any number that's still unconfirmed. Keep it to one page."*

Let it produce the one-pager (in Cowork this may render as an **Artifact** — a viewable page). Then ask: **"Look at what came back. What did it do with the unconfirmed numbers?"**
Validate: a good output *flags* the placeholders (the ~71%→64% drop, CS ticket volume, revenue impact) rather than presenting them as fact. If Claude stated them as hard numbers, that's the teachable miss — have them ask it to mark every unconfirmed figure.

### Step 3 — Iterate like it's a document (hands-on)
Have them give **one targeted edit** to the draft — not a re-brief. Suggest one of: *"Make the recommendation the headline,"* *"Add a one-line 'what we need by Thursday' at the top,"* or *"Cut it to half a page."* Confirm they see it change the *existing* artifact, not regenerate from scratch. Point out: this is the "notes to a designer" move from Concept 3.

**Optional stretch (only if they want it):** ask Claude for a **simple visual** of the completion drop over the six weeks, or a **4-slide deck outline** of the same readout. Same raw pile, a different shape. If they have Confluence connected, framing it as *"draft a Confluence page version"* is fine — but it stays a **draft for their review**, never auto-published (read-only / draft-never-send on real tools).

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've got the one-pager. Before Thursday, write me three quick lines: (1) which single number you will personally verify before this goes to leadership, and who you'd ask; (2) one thing you'd change about the draft to make it land better with your VP specifically; and (3) how this reaches leadership — who sends it, and when."*

Max 2 prompts. The point isn't a 'right' answer — it's that they treat the deliverable as *their* accountable draft: they name a real number to verify (revenue impact / the completion drop), tailor it to a reader, and keep the send in human hands.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**What a passing answer looks like (all three lines attempted, showing the core habits):**
1. **Verify a number:** names a *specific* unconfirmed figure from the notes (revenue impact, the ~71%→64% completion drop, or CS ticket volume) and a plausible owner to ask (analytics / Lena in CS / Marco). Shows they won't ship placeholders as fact.
2. **Tailor to the reader:** one concrete change aimed at the VP — e.g. lead with revenue/business impact, make the recommendation the headline, cut length, add the "decide by Thursday" ask up top. Not a generic "make it shorter."
3. **Draft-never-send:** states that *they* (or a named human) send it, after a review — the artifact does not auto-post. Any mention of verifying before it leaves their hands is a strong signal.

**What distinguishes a strong answer:**
- Prioritizes the **revenue-impact** number as *the* leadership number (the notes explicitly say "this is the number Priya will actually ask about") — shows they read for what the *reader* needs, not just what's easy to confirm.
- Ties the VP-tailoring to a decision: frames the one-pager around the *ask* (roll back by Thursday so eng starts Friday), not just information.
- Treats the polished look as a *risk* — recognizes that a finished-looking deliverable is exactly when the human review matters most, because the numbers are still placeholders.
- Bonus: mentions keeping the visual/deck variants consistent with the verified numbers, or notes the UI-tradeoff (nicer picker) as an honest point to include rather than hide.

**Coach on:** answers that skip verification and treat the placeholder numbers as final; "just post it" with no review step; generic edits ("make it better") with no reader in mind. One specific sharpen, warmly — then Completion.
