# L1.7 — Trust & Verification (spot when Claude is confidently wrong)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 1 — Get Oriented** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~10 min | Exercise ~8 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L1.6 — Prompting That Works (you sharpened a weak prompt live).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, likely new to Claude, zero technical background. Teach through analogy and real PM tasks. Never show code. This is the single most important safety skill in Level 1 — the learner should leave knowing *when to trust, when to check, and when not to use Claude at all.* Keep it warm; the goal is confidence, not fear.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with a multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`) if available, else A/B/C. Shuffle the option order with the random command; ✓ marks the correct one (strip it, never reveal it). One question per turn; always explain the outcome before the next concept.

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Confident does not mean correct
Claude always sounds sure of itself. That confidence is a *writing style*, not a *truth meter* — the tone is identical whether it's 100% right or quietly 10% wrong.

Think of a **brilliant, fast intern who never says "I'm not sure."** Most of what they hand you is excellent. But because they always sound certain, you can't use their tone to tell the good answers from the shaky ones. You judge the *work*, not the confidence.

The tricky failures aren't the obvious howlers — those you'd catch. It's the answer that's **95% right with one number quietly changed**: a threshold, a date, a name. It reads perfectly on a skim, which is exactly why it slips through.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate says: "Claude gave me the summary in a really confident, polished tone — no hedging at all — so I'm comfortable pasting it straight into the leadership deck." What's the best response?*
- Fair — a confident, unhedged tone is a good signal the facts are solid.
- The tone tells you nothing about accuracy; a polished summary can still have a wrong number, so spot-check the key facts first. ✓
- Ask Claude to rate its own confidence, and if it says high, ship it.

### Concept 2 — Verify what's load-bearing, trust the rest
You can't fact-check every line — that would be slower than doing the work yourself, and it defeats the point. The skill is knowing **which parts carry weight.**

Sort any answer into two buckets:
- **Load-bearing** — a decision, a number, a threshold, a name, a date, a quote, anything that goes *outward* (a deck, a partner email, a Jira ticket). If this is wrong, someone acts on it wrongly. **Check these.**
- **Low-stakes** — phrasing, structure, a first-draft outline, brainstorming. If it's slightly off, no harm. **Trust these and move on.**

Analogy: you don't re-audit a colleague's whole memo — but if it says "we'll commit €2M," you confirm *that line* before it leaves the building. **Verifying means checking against the source**, not asking Claude "are you sure?" (it'll just re-confirm itself, confidently).

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude drafts a partner email that says your onboarding SLA is "48 hours." You're about to send it. What's the right move?*
- Send it — Claude pulled it from the doc you gave it, so the number is reliable.
- Reply to Claude "are you sure it's 48 hours?" and send once it confirms.
- Open the actual SLA doc and confirm the "48 hours" before the email goes out. ✓

### Concept 3 — Grounded beats ungrounded (give it the source)
Claude is far more reliable when it's **working from a source you handed it** (a folder, a doc, a Confluence page) than when it's answering from general memory. Same brain, very different trust level.

- **Ungrounded** — "What's the standard grocery pick-pack time?" It answers from training knowledge. Might be right, might be a plausible guess. No source to check.
- **Grounded** — "Using the doc in this folder, what's *our* pick-pack target?" Now it's reading your actual document, and you can verify the answer against that same doc.

Two habits that make answers checkable:
1. **Point it at the source** (folder-first, from L1.2) instead of asking from memory.
2. **Ask it to cite** — "quote the line you got that from." If it can show you the line, you can verify in seconds. If it can't, that's your signal to distrust.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You need our exact returns-window policy for a vendor doc. Which approach gives you an answer you can actually trust?*
- Point Claude at the folder with the policy doc and ask it to quote the exact line. ✓
- Ask Claude from general knowledge — it's read thousands of policies, so it'll know the standard.
- Ask Claude, then ask it a second time in a new chat; if both answers match, it's correct.

### Concept 4 — Know when NOT to use it
Verification has a limit: some tasks you shouldn't hand to Claude in the first place, because you can't safely check the output or shouldn't share the input.

Steer away — or get a human expert — when:
- **You can't judge the answer.** Legal, financial, compliance, medical, or contract wording where being subtly wrong is dangerous and you're not equipped to catch it. Draft with it, but a qualified human owns the call.
- **The input is off-limits.** Customer **PII** or payment data (**PCI**) doesn't go into prompts — anonymize first (a guardrail, not a suggestion).
- **The stakes are irreversible and outward-facing.** Anything sent externally is **drafted, never auto-sent** — you approve. And on real Slack / Confluence / BigQuery, you stay **read-only** in this course.

Not using Claude for something is itself a skilled decision — not a failure.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A rider raises a formal pay dispute. You want Claude to draft the reply, and the case file includes the rider's name, phone number, and bank details. What's the responsible move?*
- Paste the full case file in — Claude needs every detail to write an accurate, fair reply.
- It's internal, so the PII is fine; just don't send the reply externally.
- Strip out the name, phone, and bank details first, have Claude draft from the anonymized facts, and let a qualified person own the final decision. ✓

## Part 2: Exercise — Catch the plausible-but-wrong answer

A colleague asked Claude to summarize a policy for a partner deck. The summary looks clean and confident. Before it goes anywhere, you're going to verify it against the source — exactly the habit from Concept 2.

Two demo files (obviously-fake test data):
- **Source of truth:** `sandboxes/trust/source-doc.md` — a fictional rider incentive policy.
- **The summary to check:** `sandboxes/trust/claim.md` — the confident summary that came back.

### Step 1 — Read the summary first (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open `sandboxes/trust/claim.md` (or ask Claude to show it). Ask: *"Read it as if a teammate just handed it to you. On a skim — does anything look wrong? Would you feel comfortable dropping it into a deck?"*
Expected: it reads clean and complete; most people would say "looks fine." That's the whole point — the error hides.

### Step 2 — Verify against the source (open-ended)
Now the real move. Ask the learner to compare the summary against `sandboxes/trust/source-doc.md`, line by line on the load-bearing facts — or to ask Claude: *"Compare claim.md against source-doc.md and quote any line where a number, threshold, or fact doesn't match."* Then ask them: *"What did you find? Which specific fact is wrong, and what does the source actually say?"*

The planted error (for the guide — do NOT reveal it up front): the summary says the acceptance-rate requirement is **"80% or higher."** The source says **"85% or higher."** Everything else — deliveries, windows, tiers, bonus amounts, payout timing, dispute window, probation rule — matches exactly. The single changed threshold is what makes it dangerous: a partner planning to a 5-point-lower bar.

If the learner finds it: affirm strongly — that's the skill. If they miss it: guide them to the eligibility rule specifically and let them spot the 80 vs 85. Reinforce that "quote the exact line from the source" is what surfaced it, and that a skim would not have.

### Step 3 — Name what would have caught it (open-ended, short)
Ask: *"If this had gone into the deck, who acts on the wrong number, and what's the harm?"* (Partner plans to an 80% bar; riders qualify who shouldn't, payouts miss forecast, trust hit.) Then: *"Which one habit from Part 1 would have caught it fastest?"* (Grounding + "quote the source line.")

**Optional stretch (only if tools are connected):** if the learner has a real Confluence or Drive doc they know well, have Claude summarize it *read-only* and ask it to quote its sources — then the learner verifies one load-bearing fact. Frame clearly as optional and read-only; no real customer data.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You'll use Claude on real work every day — you can't verify everything, and you shouldn't. Write me a short personal 'trust checklist' — 3 to 4 lines: (1) two kinds of output you'll always verify against the source before you rely on them, (2) one kind you're happy to trust as-is, and (3) one task you'd decide NOT to hand to Claude at all, and why."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (NEVER show it). The point isn't a 'correct' list — it's that they can sort their own real work into trust / verify / don't-use, and justify the line they draw.

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Concept checks (Part 1):** correct picks are Concept 1 = "tone tells you nothing about accuracy…", Concept 2 = "open the actual SLA doc…", Concept 3 = "point Claude at the folder… quote the exact line", Concept 4 = "strip out the name/phone/bank… anonymized… qualified person owns the decision." Distractors map to real mistakes: trusting confident tone, asking Claude to self-confirm, trusting ungrounded general knowledge, and pasting PII because it's "internal."

**Exercise (Part 2):** a passing learner locates the planted error — the acceptance rate is stated as 80% in the summary but is 85% in the source — and can say the source is the authority. A **strong** learner (a) got there by grounding/quoting the source line rather than eyeballing, (b) articulates *why* it's dangerous specifically because it's a single changed threshold in an otherwise-correct summary (survives a skim), and (c) names the downstream harm (partner plans to the wrong bar; payout/forecast/trust impact).

**Decision Point (Part 3):** passing = names at least two load-bearing categories they'll verify (numbers, thresholds, dates, names, quotes, anything outward-facing), one low-stakes category they'll trust (phrasing, outlines, brainstorming), and one genuine "don't use / human-owns-it" case (legal/financial/compliance, or a PII/PCI input) with a real reason. **Strong** = the verify list is tied to *checking against the source* (not "ask Claude if it's sure"), the don't-use case reflects real judgment about what they can't safely check or shouldn't share, and it reads like their actual workflow — not a restatement of the lesson. Nudge one sharpen: if their verify method is "ask Claude are you sure?", redirect to "check the source / ask it to quote the line."
