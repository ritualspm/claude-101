# L3.6 — Working Agentically (give a goal, let it run)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 3 — Power Moves** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~10 min | Exercise ~8 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L3.5 — Composition (skills + agents + tools in one move).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, comfortable with Claude by now but used to *chatting* — asking one thing, reading the reply, asking the next. This lesson flips that: you hand over a whole multi-step job at once. They have zero technical background. Teach through the feeling of **delegating to a capable colleague**, never through code or config. Anchor everything to real PM work.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with a multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`) if available, else A/B/C. Correct option marked ✓ (never shown). Shuffle option order with the random command; one question per turn; always explain the outcome before the next concept.

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Chatting vs. delegating
So far you've mostly **chatted**: you ask, Claude answers, you ask the next thing. You're steering every turn. That's like standing over someone's shoulder telling them each keystroke.

**Working agentically** is different. You hand over the *whole job at once* — a **goal**, not a next step — and Claude figures out the sequence itself: read these files → find the patterns → cross-check the backlog → write the summary. It runs the steps, then reports back. You review the result, not every keystroke.

Think of it like the difference between **dictating an email word by word** and **saying to a trusted colleague: "Pull together the rider feedback and give me the top themes by end of day."** The second one is delegation. You describe the outcome and the boundaries; they own the middle.

That's the whole shift this lesson teaches: stop narrating steps, start handing over goals.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You've got 20 minutes and three folders of feedback to synthesize. Which framing gets the most out of Claude working agentically?*
- Ask it one narrow question at a time and feed it the next file only after you've read each answer.
- Give it the goal and the folder up front — "read everything in here, pull the recurring themes, write me one summary" — and let it run the steps. ✓
- Paste all the raw text into a single message and ask "what do you think?" with no goal or output specified.

### Concept 2 — A good delegation is scoped
Handing over a goal only works if the job is **scoped** — bounded enough that Claude has everything it needs and can't wander into places it shouldn't. A great delegated task has three things:

- **A clear outcome** — "produce one summary file with the top themes," not "look into onboarding."
- **A boundary** — *which* files, *which* folder. "Everything in `sandboxes/agentic/`" is a fence Claude stays inside.
- **The guardrails baked in** — "draft only, don't message anyone," "read-only, don't change the source files." You state the limits as part of the ask.

A vague, unbounded goal ("fix our onboarding") is the delegation that goes wrong — too open, no fence, no defined "done." The skill isn't writing clever prompts; it's **describing the job the way you'd brief a good contractor**: here's the outcome, here's the material, here's what you must not do.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate says: "Agentic work is risky — you just turn Claude loose and hope." What's the best correction?*
- The safety comes from **scoping** — a clear outcome, a defined set of files, and stated limits like draft-only and read-only. A well-scoped job isn't "turned loose." ✓
- They're basically right; that's why you should only ever chat one step at a time and never delegate.
- The risk is real but unavoidable; you accept it because agentic work is faster.

### Concept 3 — Plan mode: approve before it acts
You don't have to choose between "watch every step" and "hope for the best." **Plan mode** is the middle ground. Claude first tells you *what it intends to do* — the sequence of steps — and **waits for your approval before doing any of it.** You read the plan, and only when you say go does it execute.

Think of it like a colleague sending you a **quick plan-of-attack message before they start**: "Here's how I'll tackle it — sound right?" You catch a wrong assumption *before* the work happens, not after.

In Cowork you'll see the proposed plan and an approve action. (In the terminal, **Shift+Tab** toggles plan mode.) For anything that touches real tools or matters, plan mode is the habit: **see the plan, approve, then let it run.** And because your work is draft-never-send and read-only on real data, the plan is also where you confirm nothing crosses those lines before it happens.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You're about to delegate a multi-step task that will touch a few connected tools. What's the smart way to stay in control without babysitting every step?*
- Skip planning and watch the whole thing execute live so you can hit stop if needed.
- Use plan mode — have Claude lay out the steps first, read the plan, approve it, then let it run. ✓
- Break the job into ten tiny chat messages and approve each one manually.

### Concept 4 — Review the result like you'd review a colleague's work
Delegating doesn't mean trusting blindly. When the task finishes, you're the reviewer. Two things to check every time:

- **Did it stay in bounds?** It only touched the files you named, it drafted rather than sent, it didn't alter your source material. (This is exactly why scoping and plan mode matter.)
- **Is the output actually right?** Spot-check the substance — do the themes match what's really in the files, are the counts plausible, did it invent anything? Same standard you'd hold a teammate's draft to.

Agentic work makes you faster because it does the *middle* for you. But the **judgment at the end stays yours** — and on Sonnet by default, most of this work is fast and cheap enough to run, review, and re-run without a second thought.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude finishes a delegated task and hands you a tidy summary. What's the right next move before you use it?*
- Forward it straight to your squad — the whole point of delegating was to not have to review it.
- Trust it fully this time, and only start reviewing once it's made a visible mistake.
- Spot-check it against the source files: did it stay in bounds, are the themes and counts real, did it invent anything? ✓

## Part 2: Exercise — Delegate a real multi-step task

The learner hands Claude ONE goal and watches it run end-to-end on local, fake demo files. Everything lives in `sandboxes/agentic/`.

**Set the scene for the learner:** *"You're PM on the Rider App Onboarding squad. You've collected feedback from three sources — interviews, support tickets, app-store reviews — plus your current backlog, all sitting in one folder. Instead of reading it all yourself, you're going to delegate."*

### Step 1 — Look at what you're handing over (open-ended, short)
Have them open `sandboxes/agentic/` and glance at the files: `rider-interviews.md`, `support-tickets.csv`, `app-store-reviews.md`, `backlog.md`. Ask: **"Before delegating — in your own words, what makes this folder a *good* thing to hand off as one job?"** Validate: it's self-contained (everything needed is inside), it has a clear boundary (one folder), and there's an obvious outcome (a summary of the themes). If they miss the boundary idea, point it out.

### Step 2 — Write the delegation (the core action)
Ask them to give Claude a single, scoped instruction — encourage their own words, but it should contain the three parts from Concept 2. A good version:

> *"Read every file in `sandboxes/agentic/`, find the recurring rider-onboarding pain points, count how often each shows up across the three feedback sources, note which are already covered in `backlog.md`, and write a single `SUMMARY.md` in that folder. Draft only — don't message anyone, and don't change the source files."*

**If the tutor is running live:** actually do it — run the task on the sandbox files and produce `SUMMARY.md`. Before executing, **show the plan first** (Concept 3): the steps you intend to take, and confirm you'll stay read-only on the sources and draft-only. Get their "go," then run it. Note out loud that this is exactly plan mode.

### Step 3 — What did it do? (open-ended, short)
Once `SUMMARY.md` exists, ask: **"Look at the summary. What are the top 2–3 pain points it surfaced, and — the reviewer's question — did it correctly flag which ones are NOT yet in the backlog?"** (In the demo data, the loudest themes are **ID-upload failures**, **payments/earnings never explained**, and **equipment/bag-pickup confusion**; the last two are the ones missing from `backlog.md`.) Affirm the reviewer instinct: they just did the end-of-task judgment step.

### Step 4 — The scope check (open-ended, short)
Ask: **"Did the task stay in bounds — did it only touch this folder, draft rather than send, and leave your four source files unchanged?"** This is the boundary half of Concept 4 made concrete. Confirm it did, and connect it back: that's *why* the instruction named one folder and said "draft only."

**Optional stretch (only if they want it, and framed as read-only):** the same delegation pattern works on live tools — "read the last week of #rider-onboarding-feedback and summarize the recurring complaints." Frame it strictly as **read-and-summarize**, draft-never-send, and remind them to keep any real customer PII out of the prompt. The sandbox run is the required part; the live tool is the stretch.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've now delegated a whole multi-step job instead of chatting it out step by step. Write me a short **delegation brief** (4–6 lines) for a real, recurring task from your own world that you'd hand to Claude agentically. Include: (1) the goal/outcome, (2) the boundary — which files, folder, or channel it may touch, (3) the guardrails you'd state up front, and (4) whether you'd use plan mode, and why or why not."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (never show it). The point isn't a perfect brief — it's that they can scope a real job, name its boundary, and bake in the guardrails without being reminded.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**What a passing answer looks like (Decision Point):**
- Picks a genuinely *multi-step* task suitable for delegation (not a one-line chat question).
- States a clear **outcome/deliverable** (e.g. "one summary doc," "a drafted ticket set").
- Names a **boundary** — specific files, a folder, or a channel Claude may touch — rather than an open-ended "look into X."
- Includes at least one **guardrail** correctly: draft-never-send, read-only on real data, or no PII/PCI in the prompt.
- Makes a defensible **plan-mode call** (using it when the task touches tools or matters; optionally skipping it for a trivial, fully-sandboxed local job).

**What distinguishes a STRONG answer:**
- The scoping is tight enough that you could hand the brief to someone else and they'd know exactly what "done" looks like.
- Guardrails are woven in as natural habit, not tacked on — e.g. "read-only, summarize into a draft I'll review" rather than "also don't break rules."
- Shows judgment about *when* to delegate vs. chat — recognizes agentic work pays off on repeatable, bounded, multi-step jobs, and that the review-at-the-end is still theirs.
- Bonus: mentions Sonnet-by-default or that they'd spot-check the output against the sources before using it.

**Common misses to gently correct:**
- A vague, unbounded goal ("improve our onboarding") — push for outcome + boundary.
- Forgetting guardrails entirely, or naming a task that would *send* something or expose real PII/PCI.
- Choosing a task that's really a single-step chat question — note that delegation shines on multi-step work.
