# L3.4 — The Leadership Panel (stress-test with three minds at once)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 3 — Power Moves** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~10 min | Exercise ~8 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L3.3 — Agents & Personas (you built a single persona and ran a plan by them).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, non-technical, comfortable in Cowork by now. In L3.3 they learned to simulate *one* stakeholder. This lesson scales that to *three at once* — a panel. Teach through analogy and real review dynamics they already know. Never show code. A "persona" is just a short description file that tells Claude *who to be*.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with a multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`) if available. Shuffle the options with the random command before presenting; correct option marked ✓ (never shown, strip it). One question per turn; always explain the outcome before teaching the next concept.

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — A panel is a room, not a single advisor
In L3.3 you asked one persona — say your VP — to react to a plan. Useful, but one-sided. Real decisions at work survive a **room**: the VP wants the growth story, Engineering wants to know what it costs to build, Finance wants the margin math. Each pulls in a different direction, and the *tension between them* is where the risk hides.

A **Leadership Panel** is just that room, simulated. You hand Claude **several persona files at once** — three short descriptions of who each person is and what they care about — plus a proposal. Claude then answers *as each of them in turn*, then steps back and tells you where they agreed and where they clashed.

The point isn't three nicer opinions. It's **surfacing the disagreement before you're standing in the actual review**.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A colleague says: "I already ran my proposal past my VP persona and it looked solid — so a panel would just tell me the same thing three times." What's the best correction?*
- They're right — if the VP persona approved, three personas would only pile on more approval.
- A panel isn't three copies of one view — it's three *different* lenses (growth, feasibility, margin), and the value is the clash between them, not the agreement. ✓
- A panel is only worth it for exec-level decisions; for a normal proposal one persona is the correct tool.

### Concept 2 — Distinct personas beat generic "critics"
A panel only works if the members actually **disagree by design**. If you ask Claude for "three people who will critique this," you get three flavours of the same polite feedback. The trick is to give each persona a **real axis of concern** so they naturally collide:

- The **VP** pushes on strategy and the growth narrative.
- The **CTO** pushes on feasibility, effort, and what might quietly break.
- The **CFO** pushes on unit economics and break-even.

Written well, they *can't* all agree — the CFO will worry about the exact margin case the VP is willing to bet on. That built-in friction is the whole product.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want a panel that genuinely stress-tests your idea. Which setup will do that best?*
- Three personas each told to "be a tough critic and find problems," with no other detail.
- One senior persona asked to role-play all three viewpoints in a single voice, to keep it consistent.
- Three personas each anchored to a *different* concern — strategy, feasibility, margin — so they naturally pull against each other. ✓

### Concept 3 — The synthesis is the deliverable, not the transcript
When the panel runs, you get two things: each persona's individual take, and then a **synthesis** — Claude stepping out of character to summarise *where they agreed, where they clashed, and what you'd need to resolve before proceeding*.

The synthesis is what you actually use. Three separate monologues are just raw material; the value is the map of agreement and tension on top. A good synthesis reads like meeting notes a sharp facilitator would write: "All three back a time-boxed test. VP and CFO split on the break-even assumption. CTO's concern about the pricing engine is unresolved and gates everything."

If Claude only hands you three opinions and stops, **ask it to synthesise** — that step is the point.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *The panel gives you three thoughtful individual reactions and stops there. What's the right next move?*
- Take the three reactions as the output — reading all three yourself is the honest way to weigh them. 
- Ask it to step out of character and synthesise: where do the three agree, where do they clash, what's unresolved. ✓
- Pick whichever of the three you agree with most and move forward on that one.

### Concept 4 — A panel pressure-tests; it does not decide
This is the guardrail that keeps a panel useful and honest. The personas are **simulations built from short descriptions** — they're a rehearsal room, not the real Priya, Diego, or Mara. They're brilliant for catching the obvious objection *before* the real meeting, sharpening your framing, and finding the question you hadn't answered.

They are **not** a substitute for the actual decision, the real stakeholders' sign-off, or real data. Use the panel to *arrive prepared* — then have the real conversation. And keep good habits intact: run the panel on **Sonnet** by default — it's plenty for this, and you save Opus for genuinely hard problems; personas run on **fake or already-shared** material, no customer PII or payment data in the proposal; the sandbox docs are for **reading** only, never writing back; and any output stays a **draft for you** — nothing gets sent to the real VP on the panel's say-so.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *The panel unanimously "approves" your proposal. How should you treat that?*
- As sign-off — three senior personas agreed, so you can proceed and tell the team it's approved.
- As a green light to skip the real review, since the objections were already covered.
- As a strong rehearsal result — you're better prepared, but the real decision and real sign-off still have to happen. ✓

## Part 2: Exercise — Convene the panel

Hands-on. There's a ready-made panel in the sandbox so this runs without any live tools or real data. Four fake files:

- `sandboxes/panel/proposal.md` — a fake "free delivery over €15 in Lisbon" test proposal.
- `sandboxes/panel/vp.md` — Priya, VP of Product (strategy & growth).
- `sandboxes/panel/cto.md` — Diego, CTO (feasibility & risk).
- `sandboxes/panel/cfo.md` — Mara, CFO (margin & break-even).

Everything is obviously fictional — safe to experiment freely.

### Step 1 — Meet the room (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open the three persona files (or you read them the essentials). Ask: *"Skim the three personas. In one line each — what's the ONE thing each of them will hammer on when they see this proposal?"* Validate: VP → the growth story / clear hypothesis & kill criterion; CTO → the engineering lift and the pricing-engine risk; CFO → the break-even basket size and margin. If they blur two together, point back to the file.

### Step 2 — Run the panel (real action)
Prompt the learner to ask Claude, in their own words, something like: *"Read the three personas in sandboxes/panel/ and the proposal.md. Respond as each of them in turn reacting to the proposal, then synthesise where they agree, where they disagree, and what I'd need to resolve before proceeding."*

Run it on the sandbox files and show them the result. Then ask, open-ended: *"Read the synthesis. What do you notice — where did the three line up, and where did they collide?"* Guide toward the intended tension: likely **agreement** that a time-boxed test is reasonable, and a real **clash** on the break-even math (VP willing to bet, CFO wanting the €13-basket case modeled) plus the CTO's unresolved pricing-engine concern. There's no single right answer — reward them noticing that the disagreement is the useful part.

### Step 3 (optional stretch) — Your own third axis
Ask: *"If you added a fourth chair to this panel for a proposal you actually own, whose lens is missing — Ops? Legal? a Partner/vendor view? What one concern would they bring?"* Keep it to a sentence. (Live-tool stretch, only if they want it: point the panel at a real, already-shared internal doc — never customer PII/PCI — and keep every output a draft.)

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've seen the room argue. Now you're the facilitator. Write me a short 'go / adjust / hold' note (4–6 lines) you'd actually send your team: (1) your call on the Lisbon proposal, (2) the ONE clash from the panel that most shaped it, and (3) the single thing you'd resolve before the real review."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against `scoring-rubrics.md`. The point isn't which call they make — it's that they used the panel's *disagreement* to sharpen a real decision, and named a concrete next step rather than a vague "let's align."

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Concept checks (Part 1):** Correct on the four multiple-choice checks. The load-bearing ideas: a panel is *distinct lenses in tension*, not repeated approval (C1); personas must be *anchored to different concerns* to be useful (C2); the *synthesis* is the deliverable — ask for it if it's missing (C3); a panel *rehearses, it doesn't decide*, and guardrails still hold — Sonnet-by-default, fake/shared read-only data, no PII/PCI, draft-never-send (C4). One miss with a sound recovery after the outcome beat is fine.

**Exercise (Part 2):** A passing learner can name each persona's distinct concern (Step 1) and, reading the synthesis (Step 2), can point to at least one genuine *agreement* AND one genuine *clash* — ideally spotting that the disagreement (break-even math / pricing-engine risk) is the valuable output, not a flaw. They don't need the "right" answer, just evidence they read the tension rather than skimming for a verdict.

**Decision Point (Part 3):**
- **Passing:** A clear go/adjust/hold call, tied to at least one specific panel clash (not generic "there were concerns"), plus one concrete thing to resolve before the real review.
- **Strong (distinguishing):** Treats the panel as a rehearsal, not sign-off — the note reads as "here's what I'll be ready to defend / what I still owe an answer on," names the *specific* unresolved item (e.g. the €13-basket margin case or reversibility of the pricing change), and frames a concrete next action with an owner or a question, not "let's align." Bonus if they implicitly respect a guardrail (keeps it a draft to the team, no real data assumed).

**Score:** `strong` = crisp checks + reads the tension + a sharp rehearsal-minded note. `solid` = mostly there, call is sound but the resolve-step is a little vague. `revisit` = treats the panel as decision/approval, or misses that the disagreement is the point.
