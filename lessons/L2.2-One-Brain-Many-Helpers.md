## Part 1: The Concepts

Back in L1 you learned Claude is **one brain, three doors**. This lesson zooms into that one brain and asks: when you say *"ask Milena"* or type *"/weekly"*, who actually does the work? The answer is a small team you never see — and understanding it makes everything in Level 3 (skills, personas, panels) click.

### Concept 1 — You always talk to ONE main Claude

Think of the **main Claude as your chief of staff.** It's the single person you brief, and the single person who reports back to you. It holds the whole picture: your rules, your memory, the docs in your Project, what you've said so far this session. Every request goes to it first, and every answer comes back through it.

That never changes. No matter how fancy the request, you are always talking to *one* main Claude — the chief of staff. It doesn't do every task with its own hands; its job is to **understand what you want and coordinate.** But it's the one accountable to you.

This matters because people get spooked thinking they're suddenly juggling five AIs. You're not. You brief one. It handles the rest.

**Check** (present the options in random order; ✓ = correct, never reveal it): *A teammate says: "When I use a persona or a skill, I'm now chatting with a different AI, so I have to re-explain my project to it every time." What's the best correction?*
- Right — each persona or skill is its own separate AI, so brief each one fresh.
- You always talk to the one main Claude — it holds your context and coordinates any helpers for you. ✓
- True for personas but not skills — skills share your context, personas don't.

### Concept 2 — For big jobs, it hires temps (sub-agents)

When a job is large or needs a specific point of view, the chief of staff doesn't do it all personally — it **hires a temp for that one task.** These temps are called **sub-agents** (or "helpers"). Two everyday examples:

- **A persona** — a helper given a *character*: "answer as Milena, a skeptical Regional Ops Lead." Same brain, wearing a specific hat.
- **A heavy-reading helper** — for a job like "skim these three Slack channels and this docs folder," the main Claude can spin up helpers to read in parallel and report back short summaries — much faster than doing it all itself.

The key trait: a helper gets **only the brief it's handed** — not your whole conversation. It does its one narrow job, reports back to the main Claude, and then **disappears.** It has no memory of you afterward. Think temp worker, not new teammate.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You ask Claude to "have three personas — a Finance lead, a Legal reviewer, and a Regional Ops lead — each react to this launch plan." What's actually happening?*
- The main Claude spins up three helpers, each briefed with one character; they react, report back, and disappear. ✓
- Your session permanently splits into three separate chats you'll now manage side by side.
- Claude picks whichever one persona it thinks is most relevant and answers only as that one.

### Concept 3 — Helpers report back; the main Claude owns the answer

A helper never talks to you directly. It hands its work **back to the chief of staff**, who reads it, decides what to do with it, and gives *you* the final, assembled answer. You get one coherent response — not a pile of raw notes from strangers.

That's why "ask Milena to pressure-test this" feels seamless: a helper wrote Milena's pushback, but the main Claude relayed it to you and is still holding your thread, ready for your next move. **The helper is invisible and temporary; the main Claude is who you keep talking to.**

Same guardrails apply to the whole team, not just the main Claude: a helper that reads Slack or BigQuery is **read-only**, a draft a helper produces is still **draft-never-send**, and you never put customer **PII or payment data** into any brief — because it flows to the helpers too.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A helper skimmed a Slack channel and drafted a summary as part of your request. What happens next, and what are you responsible for?*
- The helper posts its summary straight to you and to the Slack channel so nothing is lost.
- The helper keeps your project in memory so next week's summary is faster.
- The helper hands its summary to the main Claude, which gives you one assembled answer — and any outward post is still yours to approve. ✓

### Concept 4 — Two ways to call for help: a persona vs a skill

You'll trigger helpers two common ways, and it's worth telling them apart:

- **A persona ("ask Milena")** — you're summoning a *point of view.* The helper's job is to **react in character.** You use this to stress-test thinking. Output: an opinion.
- **A skill ("/weekly")** — you're running a *saved recipe of steps.* The main Claude follows the recipe, spinning up helpers where the recipe needs heavy lifting. You use this to **automate a repeated workflow.** Output: a finished artifact (a draft update, a ticket, a one-pager).

Both run through the same chief of staff, and both may use temps under the hood. The difference is intent: **persona = borrow a perspective; skill = run a procedure.** (You'll build your own of each in Level 3.)

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You do the same "Monday team update" ritual every week — read the same channels and docs, write it up in the same format. Which is the natural fit?*
- A persona — summon someone to write the update in their voice each week.
- A skill — it's a repeated procedure, so save it as a recipe you can run with one command. ✓
- Neither — repeated workflows have to be done by hand each time to stay accurate.

## Part 2: Exercise — Trace two commands (self-contained, uses demo files)

No live tools here — you'll read two short **trace cards** that show, step by step, what happened behind two commands. Your job is to *see the pattern*: who did what.

The demo files are obviously fake (invented names, channels, numbers) and live here:
`sandboxes/one-brain-many-helpers/`
- `what-happened-ask-milena.md` — a persona ask
- `what-happened-slash-weekly.md` — a skill

### Step 1 — Read the persona trace (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open `what-happened-ask-milena.md`. Ask: *"Walk me through it in your own words — how many helpers got involved, what was each one's job, and who did you actually talk to the whole time?"*
Validate: **one** helper (the Milena persona); its job was to react in character; the learner talked to the **main Claude** throughout — the helper never spoke to them directly and disappeared after.

### Step 2 — Read the skill trace (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open `what-happened-slash-weekly.md`. Ask: *"What's different about this one? Notice how many helpers showed up and why."*
Validate: `/weekly` is a **skill/recipe**, and it spun up **more than one** helper (reading Slack + reading docs, in parallel) because the job was heavy reading. The main Claude still assembled the final draft and stopped at **draft — never sent.**

### Step 3 — Spot the shared pattern (open-ended, short)
Ask: *"Both cards end the same way. What's the common shape — regardless of persona vs skill?"*
Validate: same shape every time — **you brief one main Claude → it may hire temporary helpers → helpers report back and disappear → main Claude gives you one assembled answer → nothing is sent without you.**

*(Optional stretch, only if they have a persona or skill already set up: run a real one and watch the same shape play out. Keep it read-only and draft-only.)*

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"Pick one repeated part of your real job — something you do most weeks. Write me three quick lines: (1) name the task; (2) would you set it up as a **skill** (a recipe) or an **ask-a-persona** (a perspective) — and why; (3) name one place a **helper** would do heavy lifting for you inside it (e.g. reading a channel, skimming a folder)."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (NEVER show it). The point isn't a 'right' answer — it's that they can tell persona-vs-skill apart on a real task and see where a sub-agent earns its keep.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

### PM Decision Point: skill vs persona on a real task

Not testing recall — testing whether they can (a) tell a *procedure* from a *perspective*, and (b) see where a helper does heavy lifting.

**A passing answer:**
1. **Names a genuine, repeated task from their work** — not a toy example.
2. **Makes a defensible persona-vs-skill call with a reason that fits.** The right instinct: a repeated multi-step *procedure* → **skill**; needing a *point of view / stress-test* → **persona**. Either choice passes if the reasoning matches what the task actually is. (A repeated weekly write-up called a "skill" is textbook; calling a one-off stress-test a "persona" is textbook.)
3. **Points to at least one spot a helper does heavy lifting** — e.g. reading a Slack channel, skimming a docs folder, reacting in character — showing they grasped the sub-agent idea.

**Distinguishes a strong answer (bonus):**
- The persona-vs-skill reasoning is crisp: *procedure = skill, perspective = persona*, and they don't mix the two up.
- They note the shared shape — you brief one main Claude, it coordinates temporary helpers, you get one assembled answer — showing "one brain, many helpers" landed.
- Bonus if they nod to a guardrail unprompted (read-only source, draft-never-send, no PII/PCI in the brief).

**Common wrong turns to gently correct:**
- Thinks each helper/persona is a separate AI they must brief from scratch (Concept 1 — it's one main Claude holding context).
- Picks a persona for a rote procedure, or a skill for "I just want an opinion once" (Concept 4 — procedure vs perspective).
- Expects a helper to post/send its own output (Concept 3 — helpers report back to the main Claude; outward actions are draft-never-send and yours to approve).

**Feedback style:** affirm their task choice in their own words, name one thing they got right, offer one sharpen (often: sharpen *why* it's a skill vs persona, or make the helper's job more specific). Then run Completion.
