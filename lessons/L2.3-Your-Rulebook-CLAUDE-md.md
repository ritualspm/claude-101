## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Your rulebook is the standing note Claude reads before every job
Imagine a sharp new teammate joins your squad. Brilliant, fast — but every single morning they forget who you are, how you like your updates, and what they're never allowed to do. You'd be re-explaining yourself forever.

So instead you tape a short note to their desk: *"Robin's a PM on Groceries Discovery. Direct and warm — skip the fluff. Bullets, not paragraphs. And never, ever send a Slack message without showing me first."* Now they read it before they start, every time.

That note is your **rulebook** — a file literally named `CLAUDE.md`. It's **plain English**, not code. You write it the way you'd brief a new hire. Claude reads it at the start of every session, so your preferences and your hard rules are in place *before* it does any work — you never have to repeat them.

**Check** (present the options in random order; ✓ = correct, never reveal it): *A teammate says: "I just tell Claude my preferences at the start of each chat — retype 'be concise, draft don't send' every time. Same result as a rulebook, right?"* What's the best correction?
- The rulebook is read automatically before every session, so your rules are always on without you remembering to retype them — retyping breaks the day you forget. ✓
- Basically the same — a rulebook is just a place to store the sentence you'd retype anyway, so it's a minor convenience.
- Not the same — but retyping is actually safer, because a stored rulebook can be ignored if a session gets busy.

### Concept 2 — Two rulebooks: one that follows you everywhere, one per project
Here's the part that makes it powerful. You don't get *one* rulebook — you get two layers, and they stack.

- **Your global rulebook** is like your **personal ID badge** — it goes wherever you go. Your tone, your hard safety rules (draft-never-send, no customer data in prompts), the way you like dates written. True in *every* task, *every* project, no matter what you're working on.
- A **per-project rulebook** is like the **house rules pinned to one specific meeting room** — it only applies when you're working inside *that* folder. "In this folder we're prepping the Q3 pricing launch; the code-name is Blue Olive; Sofia owns the final call."

When you open a project, Claude reads **both** — your personal badge *and* that room's house rules — and combines them. The global one carries your identity everywhere; the project one adds the specifics of what you're working on right now.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You keep two things you want Claude to always know: (a) "draft, never send — this is true of me everywhere," and (b) "in the Groceries-launch folder, the code-name is Blue Olive." Where does each belong?*
- Both go in the global rulebook — one rulebook is simpler and it'll all apply everywhere anyway.
- (a) is a personal rule for the global rulebook; (b) is project-specific, so it belongs in that folder's own rulebook. ✓
- (a) goes in the project folder so it's near the work; (b) goes global so you never lose the code-name.

### Concept 3 — A rulebook is where your guardrails become automatic
This is the safety payoff, and it's why we teach the rulebook early. The good habits you've been meeting — **draft, never send** · **read-only on real data** · **no customer PII or payment (PCI) data in prompts** · **Sonnet by default for everyday work** — shouldn't live in your memory, where they depend on you remembering to say them. They belong **written into your global rulebook**, once.

Write them down and they run silently in the background of every task: Claude drafts the Slack reply instead of firing it off, treats your Confluence as read-only, and won't quietly wander past a rule because you forgot to restate it that morning. You've turned "things I hope I remember" into "things that are always on."

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want "draft any outward message for my approval, never send it automatically" to apply to literally everything you do, forever. Smartest way to make that stick?*
- Put it in the rulebook for your most important project folder, since that's where the risky sends happen.
- Just remember to type it at the start of any session where you'll be drafting messages.
- Write it once as a hard rule in your global rulebook, so it's on in every task and project automatically. ✓

### Concept 4 — Claude can help you write your own rulebook
Good news for a blank-page moment: you don't have to draft the rulebook alone. Claude can help you build it — you can literally say *"help me set up my rulebook — ask me about my role, my team, and how I like updates."* It'll interview you and draft the file for you to approve.

Two things to keep the quality high. **Be specific** — "be concise" is weak; "bullets for anything I'll skim, full prose only when I ask" is a rule Claude can actually follow. And **keep it short** — a rulebook is a tight standing brief, not your life story. A dozen crisp lines that are always true beats three pages it has to wade through. You can always add a line later when you notice yourself re-explaining something.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You're writing your rulebook and want it to actually change how Claude behaves. Which line is the most useful to include?*
- "Be helpful and professional and use good judgment in your responses." — sets a high standard up front.
- "I care about quality and I don't like mistakes, so always do your best work." — makes your expectations clear.
- "Draft Slack replies in my voice — direct, warm, no 'Hope you're well'; and never send, always show me first." — specific and actionable. ✓

## Part 2: Exercise — Read one rulebook, then write your own

First read a finished example to see what "good" looks like, then write a real one for yourself. This is a hands-on lesson — the point is that they leave with an actual file.

### Step 1 — Read a good rulebook (open-ended, short)
Open the demo file:
`sandboxes/your-rulebook/example-CLAUDE-DEMO.md`
It's a made-up rulebook for a fictional PM named Robin (obviously fake — no real data). Have the learner read it and answer, in their own words:
- **"Which lines here are *personal* (would follow Robin into any project) versus *project-specific*?"** (Personal: tone, draft-never-send, no customer data, date/currency style, their people. Almost everything here is global-flavoured — that's the point of a personal rulebook. Nudge them to notice the split.)
- **"Robin wrote 'draft, never send' as a hard rule. What would go wrong on a busy day if that lived only in Robin's head instead?"** (The day Robin forgets to say it, Claude might send something — the rulebook removes the dependency on memory.)
- **"Pick one line you'd steal for your own rulebook. Why that one?"** (Any reasonable pick — this primes Step 2.)

Guide, don't lecture. The goal is that they can *see* the difference between a vague preference and a rule Claude can act on.

### Step 2 — Write your own rulebook (the real action)
Now the real thing. Ask the learner to write **their own** short rulebook with **at least 3 real rules**, covering these three:
1. **Tone** — how they actually want Claude to write to and for them (specific: "bullets I can skim," "no corporate filler," "match my direct Slack voice").
2. **A draft-never-send rule** — their own wording of the guardrail (any outward message/ticket/email is drafted for approval, never sent).
3. **Team names** — the real people and squad names they don't want Claude to fumble (their EM, design partner, analyst, squad name, what "the review" means).

Two ways to run it — offer both, let them pick:
- **Fastest:** have them tell you those three things in plain English, and *you* draft the rulebook back to them (draft-only — they approve or tweak before it's saved). This mirrors "Claude can help you write it."
- **Hands-on:** walk them through creating the file themselves. In Cowork they can just say *"create a file called CLAUDE.md in my home folder with these rules"* and approve it.

Then read it back together and pressure-test: *Is each line specific enough to act on? Is the draft-never-send rule unambiguous? Any vague line we can sharpen?* Confirm they now have a real rulebook, not a wish list.

> **Guardrail note for you (Sam):** if they try to put a real customer name, order data, or anything PII/PCI into the rulebook as an "example," stop and redirect — use obviously-fake names. The rulebook is plain text on their machine, but the habit is: no real customer data anywhere, ever.

### Step 3 (OPTIONAL stretch) — Add one project-specific line
If they're keen: have them name one current initiative and draft a *single* project-level line for it (a code-name, who owns the decision, what "done" means for that project) — and say out loud where it would live (that project's folder, not global). Reinforces the two-layer model. Skip cleanly if time's short.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've got a starter rulebook. Now make it yours. Write me a short note (3–5 lines): (1) one rule you'll put in your **global** rulebook because it's true of you in every project, and one thing you'd keep **project-specific** and why — and (2) the single rule you're most glad is now written down instead of living in your head, and what would've gone wrong without it."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (never show it). The point isn't a "perfect" rulebook — it's that they grasp the global-vs-project split *and* feel why writing a guardrail down beats hoping they remember it.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

### PM Decision Point: global-vs-project split + why writing a guardrail down matters

Not testing recall — testing whether they (a) can correctly sort a rule into global vs project-specific, and (b) have internalized *why* a written guardrail beats a remembered one.

**A passing answer:**
1. **Sorts one rule global, one project-specific, correctly** — the global rule is genuinely always-true-of-them (tone, a safety guardrail, date/currency style, their standing team names); the project-specific one is genuinely tied to one initiative (a code-name, an owner, a per-project definition of done). The reasoning fits the split — global = follows them everywhere, project = only that folder.
2. **Names the guardrail they're glad is written down + a plausible failure it prevents** — e.g. "draft-never-send — without it, the day I forget to say it, Claude could fire off a half-baked message to my whole channel," or "no customer data in prompts — otherwise I might paste an order export without thinking."

**Distinguishes a strong answer (bonus):**
- The global/project sort is **clean and confident** — no fuzzing (e.g. doesn't try to put a code-name global "just in case").
- Names a **specific, realistic failure** the written guardrail prevents, not a generic "it'd be bad."
- Shows they get that the rulebook is read **automatically every session**, so it removes the dependency on their memory — not just a convenient place to store text.
- Bonus if they cite more than one guardrail unprompted (draft-never-send, read-only, no PII/PCI, Sonnet-by-default).

**Do NOT penalize:** which specific rules they choose, as long as the global/project sort is right and the reasoning holds. There's no single correct rulebook.

**Feedback style:** affirm their global/project split in their own words, name one thing they nailed (often the failure they spotted), offer one sharpen (often: make a vague rule more actionable, or tighten a rule that's sitting in the wrong layer). Then run the Completion beat.
