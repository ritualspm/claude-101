# L1.3 — Search Your World (Slack, Confluence & email in plain English)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 1 — Get Oriented** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~10 min | Exercise ~8 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L1.2 — Folder-First (summarize a pile of docs).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, likely new to Claude. In L1.2 they pointed Claude at a *folder* and asked "what's in here?" This lesson extends that same instinct to the places their day actually lives — **Slack, Confluence, email** — where decisions get made and then lost. Teach through analogy and real PM tasks. Never show code. Anchor everything to "I need to catch up / find that decision."

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with one multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`), correct option marked ✓ (never shown, and shuffle the order at runtime). One question per turn; always explain the outcome before teaching the next concept.

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Same "point at it and ask" move — now on your tools, not just folders
Last lesson you pointed Claude at a folder and asked plain questions about it. Reading Slack, Confluence, or email is the **exact same move** — the "folder" is just a channel, a space, or your inbox instead.

The analogy: think of a **new joiner** who can instantly read every message in a channel and every page in a space, then tell you *"here's the gist, here's the decision, here's who owns it."* You don't teach them to search — you just ask in plain English: *"What did #proj-checkout-revamp decide about the first experiment?"*

For this to work, the tool has to be **connected** (you'll set that up properly in Level 2). Until then, Claude can't magically see your real Slack — but it can do the identical job on an **exported copy**, which is exactly what this lesson uses. The skill you're learning is the *asking*, and that's the same whether the source is live or exported.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate says: "Searching Slack in Claude is a totally different skill from summarizing a folder of docs — I'll learn it separately later." What's the best correction?*
- Right — connected tools work on a different principle, so treat it as a separate skill for later.
- It's the same move — point Claude at a source (channel, space, or folder) and ask in plain English; only the source changes. ✓
- True, but only because Slack messages are shorter, so you have to phrase questions differently.

### Concept 2 — Ask for the *decision*, not the *transcript*
The rookie mistake is asking *"summarize this channel"* and getting back a tidy retelling of everything everyone said — which is almost as long as reading it yourself. That's a **transcript**, not an **answer**.

The PM move is to ask for the **shape of what you need**:
- *"What was decided, by whom, and what's still open?"*
- *"Give me the 3 decisions and the owner of each."*
- *"I missed two weeks — what changed and what needs me?"*

Same source, radically more useful output. You're telling Claude what *job* to do (extract decisions + owners + open threads), not just *"read this."* A summary tells you what was said; a decision-hunt tells you what to *do*.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You've been out for two weeks and open a busy 200-message channel. Which ask gets you back up to speed fastest?*
- "Summarize this channel." — the clean all-purpose starting point.
- "List every message with its timestamp so I don't miss anything." — thorough is safest after time off.
- "What decisions were made while I was out, who owns each, and what still needs my input?" ✓

### Concept 3 — Trust it, then verify the load-bearing bits
Claude reading your channel is fast and usually right — but it can **misattribute a decision, miss a later reversal, or blur two similar threads.** In a channel, people change their minds; the *last word* often overrides an earlier one.

So treat its answer as a **fast, well-organized first pass**, not gospel. For anything load-bearing — a date, a dollar figure, who owns something — **click through to the source message and confirm.** A good habit: ask Claude to **quote the exact line or point you to the message** its answer is based on, so verifying takes seconds. This is the same "trust but verify" instinct you'll go deep on in L1.7 — here it's just muscle memory: *the summary is the map, the source is the territory.*

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude tells you a launch is set for 9 June, pulled from a long channel. It's going in a leadership update. Best next move?*
- Ask Claude to point you to the exact message, then click through — a later message may have moved the date. ✓
- Paste it straight into the update — Claude read the whole channel, so it's more reliable than you skimming.
- Ask a colleague from memory what the date was, and go with whoever sounds more confident.

### Concept 4 — Read-only, and no customer data in the question
Two guardrails that make this safe to do all day:

- **Read-only on real data.** When you point Claude at real Slack, Confluence, or email, you are **reading and summarizing** — never posting, editing, or publishing back. (Sending anything outward is *draft-never-send*, and that's a whole separate deliberate step you'll learn in L1.5.) Searching your world never changes your world.
- **No customer PII or payment data (PCI) in the question.** A channel export is fine. But if a thread happens to contain a customer's name, address, phone, or card details, **don't paste those into a prompt** — anonymize first. You're summarizing *what the team decided*, not handling *who the customer was*.

And the everyday-model habit: this kind of read-and-summarize work runs perfectly on **Sonnet** — fast and cost-sensible. Save Opus for genuinely hard problems.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A support thread you want summarized includes a customer's full name, phone number, and order address. What's the right way to use Claude here?*
- Paste the whole thread as-is — it's read-only, so nothing gets sent anywhere and the data is safe.
- Strip or mask the customer's personal details first, then ask Claude to summarize the issue and the decision. ✓
- Skip Claude entirely — any thread mentioning a customer is off-limits for summarizing.

## Part 2: Exercise — Summarize a channel & find the decision

Fully self-contained. The learner works on a **fake exported channel + Confluence page** shipped in the repo — no live tools needed. Live Slack/Confluence is an optional stretch at the end.

**The demo files (paths relative to the repo root):**
- `sandboxes/search-your-world/slack-export.md` — an exported thread from a made-up channel, **#proj-checkout-revamp**.
- `sandboxes/search-your-world/confluence-doc.md` — the matching experiment-log page.

Tell the learner up front: *"This is obviously fake data — invented names, invented numbers. The point is the asking, which works identically on your real channels once they're connected."*

### Step 1 — Catch up (open-ended, short)
Have the learner ask Claude (in their own words) to read `sandboxes/search-your-world/slack-export.md` and give them the **decisions, the owner of each, and anything still open** — the Concept 2 move, not "summarize."
Then ask them: **"In your own words — what does Claude say the team actually decided?"**
Validate against reality: experiment #1 = the **fee breakdown shown above the pay button, collapsed by default with tap-to-expand**, chosen over one-tap re-order; **owner: Tomas**; **market: QuickMart Sweden, 10% traffic, two-week read**; one-tap re-order **held for #2, not dropped**. Confirm gently; if their recap is transcript-shaped ("everyone talked about checkout"), nudge them to re-ask for *decisions + owners*.

### Step 2 — Find the specific decision (open-ended, short) — the verify beat
Here's the catch that makes this real. Ask the learner: **"When exactly does experiment #1 kick off? Ask Claude — and have it point you to the exact line it's basing that on."**
The trap: an early message sets kickoff for **9 June**, but a **later message reverses it to 16 June** (a Swedish bank-holiday week would make the read noisy). A quick read — or a lazy summary — surfaces *9 June*. The correct answer is **16 June**.
Validate: the strong learner (or a well-pointed Claude) catches the reversal and lands on **16 June**, citing the later message. If they say 9 June, don't correct yet — ask: *"Anything later in the thread change that?"* This is Concept 3 becoming a reflex.

### Step 3 — Cross-check two sources (open-ended, short)
Ask the learner to have Claude compare the Slack thread against `sandboxes/search-your-world/confluence-doc.md`: **"Does the Confluence page match what the channel decided — and is anything missing or unclear?"**
Validate: they should notice the Confluence page captures the decisions but **leaves the kickoff date pointing to a note instead of stating it**, and flags an **open question about the metric of record** (post-total abandonment vs. overall payment-step drop-off). Good learners spot that the doc is slightly behind the channel — exactly the kind of drift searching-your-world is meant to catch.

### Optional stretch — the live tools
If (and only if) the learner has Slack or Confluence connected: invite them to run the *same* ask on a real, low-stakes channel they own — **read-only**, and with **no customer PII**. Framing: "Identical move, live source. If nothing's connected, you've already learned the skill — connecting is a Level 2 step."

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've caught up on a channel and found the real decision. Now write the update you'd actually send — as a **draft**, since it's going nowhere until you approve it (L1.5). Give me three tight lines: (1) the decision for experiment #1 in one sentence, with the owner; (2) the kickoff date — the correct one — and why it moved; and (3) one thing you'd verify at the source before this goes to leadership, and how you'd check it."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (never show it). The point isn't polish — it's that they extracted the *decision* (not a transcript), caught the **16 June** reversal, and named a concrete verify step.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Passing answer:**
- **Part 2, Step 1:** Recaps the *decision*, not a play-by-play — names the fee-breakdown-above-pay-button choice as experiment #1 and that it beat one-tap re-order. Gets the owner (Tomas) or the market (QuickMart SE, 10%) roughly right. Shows they asked for decisions/owners rather than "summarize."
- **Part 2, Step 2:** Lands on **16 June** as the kickoff (or self-corrects from 9 June when nudged), and understands *why* — the later message reversed the earlier date for the bank-holiday clash. Bonus: they had Claude point to the specific line.
- **Part 2, Step 3:** Notices the Confluence page is slightly behind / vaguer than the channel (kickoff date not stated outright, and/or the open metric question).
- **Part 3:** Three lines covering (1) the decision + owner, (2) the correct kickoff (16 June) with the reason it moved, (3) a concrete thing to verify at source. Treats the output as a draft, not something to send.

**What distinguishes a STRONG answer:**
- Instinctively framed the Step-1 ask as "decisions + owners + open items," not "summarize this channel."
- Caught the 9→16 June reversal *without* the nudge, and explicitly cited the later message as the reason to trust 16 over 9.
- In Part 3, the verify step is specific and source-anchored ("click into Priya's Thu-5-Jun message to confirm the revised date" or "confirm the metric of record with Lena, since Confluence lists it as open") rather than a vague "double-check it."
- Naturally reached for read-only / draft-never-send / no-PII framing without being reminded, and treated Sonnet as the sensible default for this kind of work.

**Revisit signals:** hands back a transcript-style summary; states 9 June with no awareness it changed; treats Claude's answer as final with no verify instinct; suggests posting the update straight to the channel.
