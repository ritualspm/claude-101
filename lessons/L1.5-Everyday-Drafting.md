# L1.5 — Everyday Drafting (messages, one-pagers, meeting prep)

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Draft, never send (the one habit that keeps you safe)
Think of Claude as a **sharp junior who writes fast but never has the keys to the front door.** It can draft your Slack post, your email, your Jira ticket, your Confluence page — but the **Send / Publish button is always yours.** Nothing goes out until you read it and press it.

This isn't Claude being timid. It's the the rule: **draft, never send.** A draft you didn't like costs you ten seconds to delete. A message that auto-posted to #launch-nibble with the wrong date costs you a cleanup, a correction, and some trust.

So the everyday shape is: *"Draft me a Slack post about X"* → you read it → you tweak it → **you** paste and send. Claude writing to a real channel by itself is off the table, and that's on purpose.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You ask Claude to write a launch-slip announcement and you're happy with the result. It offers to "post it to the channel for you." What's the right move?*
- Let it post — you already approved the text, so sending it is just the same step automated.
- Have it post but only to a private test channel first, then it can push to the real one.
- Keep it as a draft; you copy it into Slack and send it yourself — sending stays your hand on the button. ✓

### Concept 2 — Iterating beats re-explaining (draft → nudge → nudge)
The magic of drafting with Claude isn't the first draft — it's the **cheap second, third, and fourth.** Treat it like editing with a colleague who never gets tired: the draft is on the table, and you just say what to change.

- *"Make it shorter — three lines max."*
- *"Add one sentence on **why** we're slipping."*
- *"Warmer opening, less corporate."*
- *"Now give me a one-line version for the leadership summary."*

You don't re-describe the whole thing each time. Claude **remembers the draft you're working on** and applies your nudge. Small, specific nudges beat one giant instruction — you can see each change land and steer from there.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude gives you a solid announcement, but it's a bit long and doesn't explain the reason for the slip. What's the most effective next step?*
- Start a brand-new task and re-write the whole request from scratch with all your requirements bundled in.
- Give two quick nudges on the draft it just made — "make it shorter" and "add a line on why" — and read the result. ✓
- Accept it as-is; asking for changes usually makes AI text worse, not better.

### Concept 3 — Feed it the situation, don't make it guess
A draft is only as good as what Claude knows. If you say *"write a launch update,"* it will invent plausible-but-generic filler. If you **point it at the real context** — your messy meeting notes, a situation file, the actual dates — it drafts something that sounds like *your* launch, not a template.

For a PM this is the everyday move: you already have the raw material (notes, a Slack thread, a doc). Hand it over and let Claude shape it. In Cowork you can literally point it at a file or folder (that's the folder-first habit from L1.2) instead of retyping everything.

**One guardrail rides along here:** the context you feed it must be **safe** — no customer PII, no payment-card data. A launch date and a bug description are fine. A spreadsheet of real customer names or card numbers is not — anonymize or leave it out. When in doubt, don't paste it.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want an announcement that actually fits your launch. You have messy notes from the sync in a file. Best approach?*
- Point Claude at the notes file and ask it to draft from what's there — real context, real draft. ✓
- Ask for a generic launch-slip template, then hand-edit every detail in yourself afterward.
- Paste the notes *plus* the customer list from the affected test baskets, so it has "full context."

### Concept 4 — Meeting prep is just drafting, pointed at a meeting
Same skill, different output. Walking into a review cold is the PM version of a nightmare. Claude turns a pile of notes into a **prep pack**: the three things you need to say, the decision you need out of the room, the one question someone will definitely ask, and a tight status line.

The move is the same as any draft — *feed the context, ask for the shape you want:* "Turn these notes into a 5-minute meeting brief: current status, the open decision, and the one hard question I should be ready for." And because it's Sonnet-shaped everyday work, keep it on **Sonnet** — fast and cheap. You'd only reach for Opus on genuinely hard, high-stakes strategy, not a meeting brief.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You have 4 minutes before a launch review and a wall of messy notes. What's the highest-leverage ask?*
- "Read these notes and tell me every detail in them so I don't miss anything." (a full read-back)
- "Turn these notes into a 5-min brief: status, the open decision, and the one hard question I'll get." ✓
- "Write the perfect strategy memo for this launch." (and switch to Opus to be safe)

## Part 2: Exercise — Draft, iterate, and prep from real notes

Self-contained. The learner works from a fake situation file — no live tools needed. There's a demo file at:

> `sandboxes/everyday-drafting/context.md`

It's an **obviously fake** situation: a launch called "Reorder in 2 taps" is slipping one week (13 Jul → 20 Jul) because of a payment bug. It includes a messy meeting-note dump at the bottom. Have the learner point Claude at it (folder-first habit) — or, if easier, tell them the situation is already loaded for this exercise.

### Step 1 — Draft it (real action)
Have the learner ask Claude something like: *"Using `sandboxes/everyday-drafting/context.md`, draft a short Slack announcement for #launch-nibble about the launch slipping to 20 Jul. Draft only — don't send."*
Produce the draft in the conversation. Then ask the learner (open-ended, short): **"Read it back — does it own the slip without drama? Anything that sounds off or too corporate?"** Validate their read; don't lecture. The point is they *notice* tone and accuracy, not that they nail it.

### Step 2 — Iterate with two nudges (real action)
Ask the learner to give two small nudges, e.g. *"make it shorter — three lines"* and *"add one line on **why** we're slipping."* Apply them to the same draft. Then ask (open-ended, short): **"What changed between the versions — and is the shorter one actually better, or did it lose something?"** There's no single right answer; you're coaching the *iterate-don't-restart* instinct.

### Step 3 — Prep the meeting (real action)
Point at the messy note dump at the bottom of the file. Ask the learner to request: *"Turn the meeting notes in that file into a 5-minute brief for the launch review — current status, the open decision, and the one hard question I should expect."* Produce it. Then ask (open-ended, short): **"Priya will ask if this moves the Q3 OKR date. Does your brief arm you for that question — and did it surface the still-open decision about staging?"**

### Optional stretch (live tool — draft only)
If the learner has Slack connected, mention they *could* have Claude create this as a **Slack draft** (never a send) in a real channel — but for this course, the sandbox version is enough. Frame it clearly: even with the live tool, it's **draft, never send**, and only ever on non-sensitive content.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've now drafted, iterated, and prepped — all without sending anything. Write me a short note (3–5 lines): (1) In your own everyday work, name **one message you actually dread writing** and how you'd draft-then-nudge it with Claude. (2) State **one line of the draft-never-send rule in your own words** — what you'll always do by hand. (3) Name **one thing you would NOT paste** into a draft prompt, and why."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (never show it). The point isn't a perfect answer — it's that they can map drafting onto their real work *and* have internalized the guardrail as a personal habit, not a rule they were told.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Passing answer** covers the three asks with real substance:
1. **A concrete personal drafting task** — a specific message/doc they actually write (a stakeholder update, a slip announcement, a Jira description, an exec summary), not a vague "emails." Bonus if they describe iterating (a nudge or two), not just a one-shot draft.
2. **Draft-never-send in their own words** — they clearly own the send/publish action themselves; Claude drafts, they press the button. Any phrasing that keeps the human on the final action passes.
3. **One thing they would NOT paste** — names something genuinely sensitive (customer names/PII, payment/card data/PCI, or unreleased confidential info) with a plausible reason. Naming PII *or* PCI specifically is a strong signal the guardrail landed.

**A strong answer** goes further: ties the draft-then-nudge habit to *saving real time* on a task they own; frames draft-never-send as a trust/safety instinct rather than a rule they're obeying; and is precise about the not-paste line (distinguishes safe context like dates/bug descriptions from unsafe like real customer data). Bonus for mentioning Sonnet-by-default or anonymizing-before-pasting unprompted.

**Needs a revisit (coach, don't fail):** treats "let Claude send it" as fine; gives only a generic task with no specifics; or can't name anything they'd withhold. Give one specific piece of praise and one sharpen, then complete the lesson.
