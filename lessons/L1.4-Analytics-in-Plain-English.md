## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — You ask the question; Claude writes the query
Getting a number out of a database normally means someone writes **SQL** — a precise, fussy query language — and runs it against the data warehouse. That's why PMs end up in a queue, waiting on an analyst for something as simple as "how many orders did Vienna do last week?"

Think of it like ordering in a restaurant. You don't walk into the kitchen and cook — you tell the waiter what you want in plain language, and the kitchen handles the rest. **Claude is the waiter who also happens to be a chef.** You ask *"orders per city for the last four weeks"* in plain English; Claude translates that into the exact query, runs it, and brings back the answer.

At work, the "kitchen" is **BigQuery**. But you don't need to know that to ask — you just need to know what you want to learn.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate says: "I can't ask Claude about our order numbers — I don't know how to write SQL." What's the best response?*
- "You don't write the query — you ask the question in plain English and Claude writes and runs the query for you." ✓
- "You're right, you'll need the SQL basics first — do a quick course, then come back."
- "Skip the database — just paste last month's numbers into the chat and ask about those."

### Concept 2 — Always ask it to show its work
Here's the catch with a helpful waiter: sometimes they bring the wrong dish and say it with total confidence. Claude can misread what you meant — pull the wrong date range, count "orders" a different way than you'd expect, or quietly leave out cancelled orders.

So the habit is: **ask for the answer *and* the reasoning.** Not the raw SQL (you don't need it) — but the plain-English version: *"which dates did you include? how did you define an order? did you filter anything out?"* If the logic behind the number is wrong, the number is wrong — and a confident wrong number in a leadership deck is worse than no number at all.

A good ask sounds like: *"Give me weekly orders by city, and tell me in plain words how you counted them and what you filtered."*

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude gives you a clean-looking number: "Vienna did 6,900 orders last week." You're about to put it in a QBR deck. Best next move?*
- Paste it in — it's a specific number with a clear source, so it's trustworthy.
- Ask Claude to explain in plain English which dates and definition it used, then sanity-check that against what you expected. ✓
- Ask Claude to run it three more times; if you get the same number, it's correct.

### Concept 3 — Follow-ups are free — start broad, then drill in
The magic of asking in plain English is that you can keep going. You don't re-brief from scratch — Claude remembers the question you just asked. So you **start with the big picture and drill down**, the way you'd actually interrogate a trend.

- *"How are total orders trending over the last five weeks?"* → sees the overall shape.
- *"Break that down by market."* → which country is driving it.
- *"Now by city, just for the market that's growing fastest."* → the specific story.

Each follow-up is one plain sentence. This is the loop that turns a dashboard you *stare at* into a conversation you *have* — and it's where the real insight usually hides (the total looks flat, but one city is quietly exploding).

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You just asked "how are orders trending overall?" and got a flat-looking total. You suspect one market is masking the others. What's the efficient next step?*
- Start a brand-new task and carefully re-describe the whole dataset and question from scratch.
- In the same conversation, say "break that down by market" — it keeps the context and drills in. ✓
- Ask for the raw table of every row so you can eyeball it yourself.

### Concept 4 — Read-only, and no customer PII in your questions
Two guardrails that make this safe to do every day:

- **Read-only on real data.** In these exercises (and as your default habit), Claude *reads and summarizes* from BigQuery — it never writes to, changes, or deletes production data. You're pulling numbers, not editing the warehouse.
- **No customer PII or payment data (PCI) in your prompts.** Aggregates — orders per city, GMV per market, week-over-week growth — are exactly what you want and are safe. But never ask things that surface an individual: a specific customer's address, phone number, or card details. If a question would expose a person, reshape it into an aggregate.

Rule of thumb: **counts and trends, yes; individual people, no.**

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Which of these data questions is the safe, appropriate one to ask Claude against the real warehouse?*
- "List the phone numbers of the 20 customers who cancelled most often in Vienna last month."
- "Pull the full names and delivery addresses for every late order yesterday."
- "Show me cancelled orders per city last month as a share of total orders." ✓

## Part 2: Exercise — Ask a real metric question

The learner works against a small, **obviously fake** demo dataset so this runs with no live tools. File:

`sandboxes/analytics/orders.csv` — columns: `date, market, city, orders, gmv_eur`. Six weeks (early May → early June 2026), three markets (Sweden, Finland, Austria), two cities each. There is a deliberate growth story hiding in it.

### Step 1 — Ask the big-picture question (real action)
Have the learner ask, in their own words, something like: *"Look at `sandboxes/analytics/orders.csv`. How are total orders trending across the whole file, week by week?"*

Guide them to expect: Claude reads the file, sums orders per week, and reports the trend in plain English. **Prompt them to also ask Claude to say how it counted** (which column, whether it summed across all markets) — that's Concept 2 in action.

*What's actually there:* total weekly orders climb steadily — but gently. Nothing dramatic at the top line. That flat-ish overall picture is the trap.

### Step 2 — Break it down by market (the follow-up)
Now the key move: *"Break that down by market."* In the **same** conversation — no re-briefing.

Ask them the open question: **"What do you notice? Is every market growing the same way?"**

*What's there to find:* Sweden and Finland are basically flat (slow, steady). **Austria is the story** — Vienna and Graz are climbing fast; Vienna nearly doubles over the six weeks. The healthy-looking total was hiding one market surging while two idled.

### Step 3 — Drill into the story (their own follow-up)
Let them chase it: *"Now break Austria down by city"* or *"show me GMV per order for Austria over time."* Ask: **"In one line — what's the headline you'd take to your team?"** (Something like: *"Austria is our growth engine right now — Vienna orders up ~65% in six weeks — while the Nordics are flat."*)

### Optional stretch (live BigQuery)
If the learner has BigQuery connected: have them ask the **same shape** of question against a real aggregate table (orders per city per week), **read-only**, and — critically — ask Claude to explain in plain English how it defined an order. Frame it as optional; the CSV is enough to learn the loop. Remind them: aggregates only, no PII.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You found that Austria is quietly driving the growth while the total looked calm. Write me a short 3-line Slack update you'd post to your squad channel: (1) the headline finding in plain English, (2) the one number that backs it up, and (3) one honest caveat — something you'd want to verify before anyone treats this as fact."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (NEVER show it). The point isn't a perfect finding — it's that they can turn a plain-English query into a clear, honest, decision-ready message, and that they instinctively flag what still needs checking. If they draft the Slack message, remind them of the habit: **draft, never auto-send** — it's theirs to review and post.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Passing answer** includes all three requested parts:
1. A plain-English headline that correctly identifies **Austria** (or Vienna/Graz specifically) as the growth driver — not "orders are up" in general, which misses the whole point of the breakdown.
2. At least one concrete supporting number pulled from the data (e.g. Vienna orders growing from ~3,600 to ~6,900, roughly +65–90%; or Austria GMV climbing while Sweden/Finland stay flat). The exact figure needn't be perfect; it must be in the right ballpark and clearly tied to the claim.
3. A genuine caveat that shows verification instinct — e.g. "only six weeks / short window," "need to confirm how 'order' is defined and whether cancellations are excluded," "is this a promo spike or real growth?", "demo data — re-run against the real warehouse read-only."

**What distinguishes a STRONG answer:**
- The caveat is *specific and data-literate* (questions the definition, the time window, seasonality/promo effects, or completeness) rather than a generic "we should double-check."
- The headline reflects the *insight from drilling down* — that the flat-looking total masked a single surging market — not just the surface number.
- Tone is squad-ready: concise, confident but honest, no false precision.
- Bonus: mentions it's a draft to review before posting, or notes it's aggregate/read-only data (guardrail awareness) without being told.

**Common misses to coach on:**
- Reporting only the top-line total ("orders are growing") — they skipped the breakdown insight.
- A confident finding with **no** caveat — coach the "confident wrong number in a deck" risk from Concept 2.
- Inventing a precise number not supported by the file — coach show-your-work / sanity-check.
