## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — Three models = three engines, and you're paying for fuel
The single mental model for this whole lesson: Claude comes in **three sizes**, and picking the size is like picking a vehicle for a trip.

- **Haiku** — a **scooter.** Fast, cheap, nimble. Perfect for short hops: reword this, reformat that, look one thing up. Terrible for hauling a heavy load.
- **Sonnet** — a **reliable car.** This is what you drive 90% of the time. Fast enough, smart enough, cheap enough for real work: summaries, drafts, tickets, search, meeting prep.
- **Opus** — a **moving truck.** Powerful, handles the heavy stuff nothing else can — and it *burns the most fuel and moves slower*. You don't take the truck to buy milk.

The "fuel" is real: bigger model = more cost and more waiting. So the choice isn't "which is best?" — the best engine is the *smallest one that comfortably does the job.* Reaching for Opus on a reword is like renting a moving truck to post a letter.

**Check** (present options in random order; ✓ = correct, never reveal it): *A teammate says: "I always pick Opus — it's the smartest, so why would I ever use a weaker model?" What's the best correction?*
- Fair point — if it's the smartest, defaulting to Opus is the safe habit.
- Opus is the moving truck: slowest and most expensive. Pick the *smallest* engine that comfortably does the job — that's usually Sonnet. ✓
- Agreed on principle, but only because Opus is also the fastest of the three.

### Concept 2 — Sonnet is the default; you *earn* your way up to Opus
Here's the house rule, and it's a habit worth building on day one: **Sonnet by default.**

Almost everything a PM does — weekly summaries, Slack drafts, Jira tickets, reading a folder of docs, prepping a meeting — is Sonnet work. It's fast, it's cheap, and it's genuinely good. You should feel a *little friction* before reaching past it.

Opus isn't "better Sonnet." It's for problems that are **genuinely hard** — deep synthesis across messy sources, thorny trade-off reasoning, the kind of thing where a smarter engine actually changes the answer. If Sonnet would get it right anyway, Opus just costs more and makes you wait. The instinct to build: *start at Sonnet, step up only when the problem earns it.*

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You're about to write your routine Monday squad summary from a folder of notes — something you do every week. Which model, and why?*
- Opus — it's an important recurring deliverable, so use the strongest engine to be safe.
- Haiku — it's routine, so use the cheapest possible engine every time.
- Sonnet — routine drafting and summarizing is exactly the 90% Sonnet is built for. ✓

### Concept 3 — Effort is the *second* dial: how hard to think, not how smart
Model is one dial. **Effort** (sometimes shown as "thinking" — low / medium / high) is a second, separate dial: it controls **how long Claude reasons before it answers**, on whichever model you picked.

- **Low effort** — snap answer. Great for simple, obvious tasks. Fast and cheap.
- **Medium effort** — the sensible default for real work. Thinks a bit, then answers.
- **High effort** — chews on it. For nuanced writing and tricky reasoning where the *thinking* is the value.

The two dials work together. A strategy one-pager might be **Sonnet + high** (smart enough, but think hard). A gnarly analysis might be **Opus + high** (bring the big engine *and* let it reason). A quick reword is **Haiku + low** (small engine, snap answer). Turning effort up on a trivial task just makes you wait for no benefit — same waste as over-sizing the model.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You need a crisp, nuanced strategy one-pager for your VP. It's not technically hard — but the wording and the judgment have to be sharp. What's the smartest setting?*
- Opus on low effort — bring the biggest engine but keep it fast so you're not waiting.
- Sonnet on high effort — Sonnet handles the writing well; turn *effort* up so it reasons hard about the nuance. ✓
- Haiku on high effort — high effort will compensate for the smaller model.

### Concept 4 — The money & speed instinct (and where safety fits)
Zoom out. Every time you pick model + effort, you're spending two things: **budget** and **your own time waiting.** The instinct to internalize:

> **Default to Sonnet + medium. Step *up* (Opus, high effort) only when the problem is genuinely hard. Step *down* (Haiku, low) when it's trivial.**

Over-sizing wastes money and time; under-sizing gives you a weak answer you have to redo. Right-sizing is a skill, and it compounds — you make this call dozens of times a day.

One thing model choice does **not** change: **the guardrails still apply on every engine.** Haiku, Sonnet, Opus — none of them get to send a Slack message on their own (draft-never-send), write to real data (read-only), or see customer PII/PCI in your prompt. A bigger engine is not more trusted; it's just more capable. Safety is a separate rule from speed.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You've moved a sensitive task from Sonnet up to Opus because it's genuinely hard. Does anything change about what you're allowed to paste into the prompt?*
- Yes — Opus is the enterprise-grade engine, so it's safe to paste real customer data into it.
- No — model choice is about capability, not trust. No customer PII/PCI goes in the prompt on *any* engine. ✓
- Yes — but only the effort level matters for data safety, not the model.

## Part 2: Exercise — Right-size a real inbox

The learner works a realistic "Tuesday inbox" of four tasks and assigns each a model + effort. Self-contained; no live tools needed.

**Point them at the demo file:** `sandboxes/model-and-effort/task-inbox-DEMO.md` (obviously fake — no real data). In Cowork they can open it, or you can walk them through it task by task.

**The model & effort table (show this to the learner as their cheat-sheet — plain language, no jargon):**

| Job feels like… | Model | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quick hop — reword, reformat, look one thing up | Haiku (scooter) | Low |
| Everyday real work — summaries, drafts, tickets, reading docs, meeting prep | Sonnet (car) — your default | Medium |
| Not technically hard, but the wording/judgment must be sharp | Sonnet (car) | High |
| Genuinely hard — messy synthesis, thorny trade-offs, conflicting data | Opus (truck) | High |

Rule of thumb baked into the table: **start at Sonnet + medium; step down to Haiku/low when it's trivial; step up to Opus/high only when the problem earns it.**

### Step 1 — Read the inbox (orient)
Ask the learner to open the file (or read the four tasks aloud with them). Confirm they can see **Task A (reword), Task B (weekly summary), Task C (strategy one-pager), Task D (gnarly analysis)**. One line each is enough — don't let them over-read.

### Step 2 — Assign model + effort to each (open-ended)
Ask: *"For each of the four tasks, give me a model (Haiku / Sonnet / Opus) and an effort level (low / medium / high) — one short line of 'why' each. Lean on the table if you want."*

Don't grade against a rigid grid — there's reasonable spread. But listen for the *shape* of their reasoning:
- **A (reword):** Haiku + low is ideal. Sonnet + low is fine. Opus = clearly over-sized (flag it warmly).
- **B (weekly summary):** Sonnet + medium — textbook default. Opus here is the classic over-reach to catch.
- **C (strategy one-pager):** Sonnet + high — smart enough, effort up for nuance. Opus + high is defensible if they justify the stakes; the key insight is *effort* high.
- **D (gnarly analysis):** Opus + high — this is the one task that genuinely earns the big engine *and* deep thinking. Under-sizing this to Sonnet + medium is the mistake to surface.

### Step 3 — Notice the pattern (open-ended, short)
Ask: *"Look across your four answers — what do you notice?"* You're steering them toward the punchline: **most tasks are Sonnet; only one earned Opus; effort moved more often than the model did.** If they say something like that in their own words, they've got the instinct. If they Opus'd everything, gently replay the "moving truck to buy milk" image.

**Optional stretch (only if they ask):** they could actually switch their own model/effort in Cowork settings before their next real task and feel the speed difference. Frame as optional — the learning is the judgment, not the clicking.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"Write me your own one-line default rule for picking model + effort — the thing you'll actually remember on a busy day. Then name one specific task from your real week where you'd deliberately break that default and reach for Opus, and say why it earns it."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against `scoring-rubrics.md` (never show it). The point isn't reciting the table — it's that they've internalized *Sonnet-by-default, step up only when earned*, and can name a real moment worth the truck.

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**What a passing answer looks like:**
- **Part 1:** Gets at least 3 of 4 checks right (or self-corrects after the outcome beat). The non-negotiable understandings: Sonnet is the default; Opus is for genuinely-hard problems, not "important" ones; effort is a separate dial from model; guardrails (esp. no PII/PCI) hold on every engine.
- **Part 2:** Assigns roughly sensible model+effort across the four tasks with *reasoning tied to task difficulty*, not vibes. A passing learner defaults most tasks to Sonnet, uses Opus at most once (Task D), and doesn't over-size the reword or the weekly summary. Perfect grid not required — coherent reasoning is.
- **Part 3:** States a personal default rule that centers Sonnet-by-default, AND names one concrete, plausible real-week task that genuinely warrants Opus with a reason grounded in *difficulty of reasoning* (not importance/seniority of audience alone).

**What distinguishes a strong answer:**
- Names the **two dials as independent** and moves effort *without* moving the model where appropriate (e.g., Sonnet + high for the strategy doc) — shows they didn't collapse "harder" into "bigger model."
- Articulates the **cost/speed trade-off** explicitly ("over-sizing wastes budget and my time; under-sizing means I redo it").
- The Opus example is *reasoning-hard* (messy synthesis, conflicting data, deep trade-offs) rather than merely high-stakes — the tell that they understood Concept 2.
- Bonus signal: spontaneously notes that guardrails don't relax on a bigger model.

**Common misses to gently correct:**
- Defaulting to Opus "to be safe" → replay the moving-truck image.
- Confusing "important task" with "hard task" → importance sets *effort*; difficulty sets *model*.
- Under-powering the genuine analysis (Task D on Haiku/Sonnet-low) → weak answer they'd have to redo.
- Assuming a bigger model is "more trusted" for sensitive data → all engines follow the same PII/PCI rule.
