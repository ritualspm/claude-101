# L1.2 — Folder-First (point Claude at a pile of docs)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 1 — Get Oriented** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~10 min | Exercise ~8 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L1 — The Three Doors (and why Cowork is home).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, likely new to Claude and possibly the terminal. Teach through analogy and their own screen. They are in Cowork right now — use that. Never show code. Anchor everything to real PM tasks (prepping a review, chasing action items, writing a status for a VP). Translate anything technical to plain language.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with one multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`), correct option marked ✓ (never shown, and shuffle the order). One question per turn; always explain the outcome before teaching the next concept.

---

## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — A folder is a workspace, not an attachment
The instinct from every other tool is: *copy the doc, paste it into the chat.* Drop that instinct here. In Cowork you don't paste documents in — you **point Claude at the folder they live in** and it reads them in place.

The analogy: it's the difference between **photocopying five pages to hand someone** versus **giving them the key to the filing cabinet.** Once Claude has the folder, it can open any file inside, cross-reference them, and notice when two documents disagree. You never re-paste, and nothing gets truncated or half-copied.

This is the single biggest everyday win for a PM. Your work already lives in folders — meeting notes, PRDs, experiment reads, emails. Point Claude at the pile and it makes sense of it.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct, never reveal it): *You have a folder with 8 documents for your initiative — notes, a PRD, two experiment reads, some emails. You want Claude to help you make sense of all of it. What's the smartest move?*
- Copy the text of all 8 documents into one long message so Claude has everything up front.
- Point Claude at the folder and let it read the documents in place. ✓
- Open the most important document, paste that one in, and describe the other seven from memory.

### Concept 2 — Start wide, then get specific
When you point Claude at a folder, resist asking your precise question first. **Start wide:** *"What's in here?"* Let it give you the lay of the land — what the documents are, what they're about, what stands out.

The analogy: you don't interrogate a new hire on day one. You first say *"walk me through what's on your plate,"* and *then* you ask the sharp follow-ups. Same here. The wide question surfaces things you'd never have known to ask about — a decision buried in a decision log, an action item that's overdue, a contradiction between the PRD and the latest test.

Then you narrow, one ask at a time:
- *"Pull out every open action item and who owns it."*
- *"What decisions have been made, and which are still undecided?"*
- *"Write me a 3-line status for my VP."*

Each answer sharpens the next question. That back-and-forth is the skill.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You've just pointed Claude at a folder of project docs you half-remember. What's the best opening question?*
- "Is this project on track to hit +3% conversion?" — go straight to the number you care about.
- "What's in here — give me the lay of the land?" then narrow from what it surfaces. ✓
- "Rewrite the PRD to be more ambitious." — start by improving the biggest document.

### Concept 3 — Trust, but verify against the source
Claude is reading real documents, so its summary is grounded in them — but a summary is still an interpretation. Before you forward a number to your VP or repeat a "decision" in a meeting, **have it point you to where in the folder that claim came from.** *"Which document says the launch is DE-first?"* — and glance at that file yourself.

The analogy: you'd never quote a figure to leadership because *a colleague mentioned it* — you'd check the source deck first. Same discipline. The load-bearing facts — numbers, dates, decisions, owners — are exactly the ones worth a ten-second check against the file.

This matters most because your reader can't see the folder. If you say "the test shows +1.9%," that number is now *yours*. Verify it before it leaves your hands.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude summarizes the folder and states "the June test shows a +3% conversion win." You're about to put that in a note to your VP. Best move?*
- Send it — Claude read the actual documents, so the number is reliable.
- Round it to "+3-4%" to be safe, then send.
- Ask Claude which document that figure came from and glance at that file before quoting it. ✓

### Concept 4 — Keep it read-only, keep it clean
Two habits that make folder-first safe at work:

- **Read-only on real data.** Pointing Claude at a folder to *read and summarize* is exactly right. But when it comes time to *act* on what it found — a Slack update, an email reply to your VP — that's **drafted for your approval, never sent automatically.** Read freely; send nothing on your behalf.
- **No customer data in the pile.** A folder of your own working docs — notes, PRDs, decision logs — is fine. A folder containing **customer names, addresses, or payment details (PII/PCI)** is not something you point Claude at. If real operational data is involved, strip the personal details first.

And a cost note: this everyday reading-and-summarizing work runs great on **Sonnet** — that's your default. Save Opus for the genuinely hard problems.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You point Claude at your project folder, it summarizes everything, and you ask it to reply to your VP's email with the status. What should happen — and what's the one thing to check about the folder itself?*
- It sends the reply for you (that's the point of automation); just make sure the folder has the latest PRD.
- It drafts the reply for your approval and sends nothing on its own; and the folder should hold your working docs, not customer names or payment details. ✓
- It saves the reply as a file in the folder and emails your whole team; make sure everyone has folder access.

## Part 2: Exercise — Make sense of a real folder

The learner does the real thing on a safe demo folder. **All files are fake — no real data.**

The folder lives at `sandboxes/folder-first/` in this course repo. It's a slice of a PM's world:
- `meeting-notes.md` — a weekly squad sync
- `mini-PRD.md` — the one-tap reorder feature
- `experiment-result.md` — an interim A/B test read
- `decision-log.md` — what's been decided / what's still open
- `stakeholder-email.txt` — the VP asking for a status by Friday

### Step 1 — Go wide (open-ended, short)
Have the learner ask Claude, in their own words: **"What's in the folder `sandboxes/folder-first/` — give me the lay of the land."**
Then ask them: *"In one line — what's this project actually about, and what surprised you?"*
Validate against reality: it's the **one-tap reorder** feature; the surprising bits are usually that the test win is *smaller than hoped* (+1.9%, Android-only, interim) and that the **DE-first vs AE-first launch decision is still open.** Affirm what they caught; nudge if they missed the "still open" part.

### Step 2 — Get specific: action items (open-ended, short)
Have them ask: **"Pull out every open action item and who owns it."**
Ask: *"Who owns the thing that's actually blocking a launch date?"*
Validate: **Priya** owns the DE-first vs AE-first launch-order call — that's the blocker (per the decision log and the meeting notes). Others: Lena (fee-on-step-1 mockups), Tom (payment latency ticket), Marco (hold iOS, recheck Android).

### Step 3 — Verify a number (open-ended, short)
Have them ask: **"The VP email mentions early test numbers — what does the experiment doc actually say, and where?"**
Ask: *"If your VP asks 'so, +3%?' — what do you honestly tell her?"*
Validate the verification instinct: the PRD *targets* +3%, but the experiment read shows **+1.9%, Android-only, interim, one weekend included** — not a clean +3%. The point of the step is that they caught the gap between the *goal* and the *result* by checking the source, not by trusting the first summary.

### Optional stretch (only if they want it)
If the learner has a real, non-sensitive folder of their own work handy (no customer PII/PCI), have them point Claude at it and run the same three moves: wide → action items → verify one fact. Frame reading real files as fine; any *sending* would be drafted for approval.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"Sofia (VP) wants a status by Friday — plain language, no jargon, and she's honest-numbers-only. Using only what's in the folder, draft her the reply. Cover the three things she asked for: (1) a one-paragraph status, (2) the launch date or a clear reason there isn't one yet, and (3) whether the early numbers justify the eng investment. Keep it to what Sofia would actually read — short."*

Remind them lightly: this is a **draft** — at work, Claude drafts outward messages for your approval, it doesn't send them.

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (never show it). The point isn't perfect prose — it's that they used the folder to produce an honest, grounded, decision-useful status.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**What a passing answer looks like:**
- The drafted status is **grounded in the folder**, not invented — it reflects the one-tap reorder feature and the real state of things.
- It **names the launch blocker honestly:** there is no launch date yet because the DE-first vs AE-first decision (Priya's, still open) is unresolved — rather than vaguely saying "soon."
- It represents the numbers **honestly:** +1.9%, Android-only, interim — not the +3% target dressed up as a result. Bonus if it notes the guardrail (AOV flat) held.
- It's **short and jargon-light** — something a busy VP would actually read.

**What distinguishes a strong answer:**
- Explicitly **separates goal from result** ("we targeted +3%; the interim Android read is +1.9% and still running") — showing they verified against the source, not the summary.
- Confirms the **fee-transparency** point Sofia raised (fee moved to step 1 / visible before payment), because they noticed she asked and checked the folder for it.
- Gives Sofia a **decision-useful line**, not just facts — e.g. "I'll bring a DE-first vs AE-first recommendation to Monday's review, which unblocks the date."
- Treats it as a **draft for approval**, not a message to fire off — reflecting the draft-never-send habit without being told again.

**Common misses to coach on (one sharpen, warm):**
- Quoting "+3%" as if it were the result → coach the goal-vs-result distinction and the verify-the-source habit.
- Promising a launch date the folder doesn't support → coach grounding claims in what's actually decided.
- A long, jargon-heavy wall of text → coach writing to the reader (a VP with four back-to-back reviews).
