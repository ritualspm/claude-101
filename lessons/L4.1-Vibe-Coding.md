# L4.1 — Vibe Coding (build a working thing by describing it)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 4 — Frontier** *(optional)* | ~23 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~11 min | Exercise ~8 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L3.2 — Find & Trust a Skill.

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, zero technical background, possibly nervous about the word "coding." That nervousness is the thing to dissolve. The whole point of this lesson: **they never write, read, or understand code — they describe an outcome in plain English and Claude builds it.** Teach entirely through the "contractor building from your description" frame. Never show a line of code, never name a language or framework. The prototype is generated *live* on obviously-fake demo material — nothing real, nothing that ships. The production/security/PII caveat is not a footnote; it's the spine of the lesson.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with a multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`) if available, else A/B/C. Correct option marked ✓ (never shown). One question per turn; always explain the outcome before the next concept.

---

## Part 1: The Concepts

You've spent this course *describing* things to Claude — drafts, summaries, skills. Here's the surprise Level 4 opens with: you can describe a **working, clickable thing** the same way, and Claude will build it while you watch. No code from you. Ever. This is often called **vibe coding** — you set the vibe (the outcome), Claude handles the machinery.

### Concept 1 — You describe the outcome, not the code

Imagine hiring a contractor to build a small shed. You don't hand them a physics textbook on load-bearing joints — you say *"a shed about this big, door on the left, shelves along the back."* You describe **what you want**; they know **how to build it.** Vibe coding is exactly that: you describe the thing you want in plain English — *"a single page that lists shifts riders want to give away, with a button to grab each one"* — and Claude writes and runs the actual machinery behind it.

The mental shift that unlocks everything: **you are not learning to code, and you never will in this lesson.** You don't read the code, you don't understand the code, you don't touch the code. You look at the *result* — the clickable thing — and judge it the way you judge any product: does it do what I pictured? That judgment is your entire job here. It's the one skill you already have.

**Check** (present the options in random order; ✓ = correct, never reveal it): *A PM says: "I'd love to mock up an interactive prototype, but I can't code, so I'll wait for engineering to have capacity." What's the best correction?*
- Fair — building anything clickable means writing code, so it does need an engineer's time.
- You don't write or read any code — you describe the outcome in plain English and Claude builds it; you just judge the result. ✓
- Half right — you can describe it, but you'll need to learn the basics of the code to fix it when it breaks.

### Concept 2 — Steer by outcome, iterate in plain English

The first version is never quite it — and that's the point, not a failure. You don't "debug." You **react like a PM at a demo:** *"the Grab button should move the shift to a 'Taken' list, not just delete it"* … *"make the whole thing a bit calmer, too much red"* … *"add a spot to type in a new shift."* Each plain-English reaction, Claude turns into a new version you can click again.

This is a **loop, not a launch:** describe → look at it → say what's off → look again. You're steering by **outcomes** (*"it should feel like X, do Y"*), never by syntax. If something's broken, you don't diagnose it — you just say *"the Grab button doesn't do anything"* and Claude fixes the underlying cause. Your leverage is describing *what good looks like*, clearly, over and over. That's the same muscle as writing a sharp acceptance criterion.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Your prototype's "Grab" button doesn't respond when clicked. You can't read code. What's the right move?*
- Open the file, find the button's code, and try to spot the mistake yourself.
- Give up on that feature — clicking-things-that-do-stuff is beyond what describing can achieve.
- Tell Claude in plain English: "the Grab button doesn't do anything when I click it" — and let it fix the cause. ✓

### Concept 3 — Prototype: yes. Production: not without review, security, and no real data

This is the line you must never blur, so hold it firmly. What you build this way is a **prototype** — a *sketch you can click*, brilliant for making an idea visible, testing a flow with your squad, or replacing "imagine a screen where…" with "look at this." That's an enormous unlock and completely safe.

What it is **not** is production. A prototype has not been security-reviewed, has not been tested against real edge cases, and has no business touching real systems. So two hard rules: **(1)** anything headed for real users goes through engineering — proper review, security, and evaluation first; a vibe-coded prototype is the napkin sketch, never the building. **(2)** you build it on **obviously-fake demo data only** — no customer or rider PII, no payment (PCI) data, no real operational exports, ever. If your idea needs real data to feel real, that's the signal to invent believable fake data — not to paste the real thing. Think of it as a **crash-test dummy: perfect for the test, never a passenger.**

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You vibe-coded a slick internal dashboard prototype. Your director says "great, let's point it at the live orders database and roll it out to ops Monday." What's the right response?*
- Sounds good — it already works with the fake data, so swapping in the real database is a quick change.
- Pause — a prototype isn't production; real data and real users need engineering review, security, and evals first. This stays a sketch until then. ✓
- Fine to roll out to ops as-is on fake data, then quietly connect the real database once people like it.

### Concept 4 — Right-sized: prototypes are cheap, so make the throwaway one

Because there's no code for *you* to maintain, the cost of trying an idea collapses to a few minutes of describing. That changes what's worth doing: instead of writing three paragraphs *arguing* for a flow in a doc, you build the clickable flow and let people react to the real thing. A prototype answers "does this even feel right?" faster than any spec.

And keep it in proportion. A quick clickable mockup to spark a squad conversation is **routine work — run it on Sonnet.** You reach for a heavier model only on genuinely hard problems, which a throwaway prototype is not. The instinct to protect: build the smallest clickable thing that makes your point, get reactions, throw it away if it was wrong. Cheap to make means cheap to be wrong — which means you try more ideas.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want your squad to react to a new onboarding flow you're picturing. What's the sharpest move?*
- Write a detailed three-page spec describing every screen so there's no ambiguity before anyone reacts.
- Build a small clickable prototype of the flow on fake data so they react to the real thing, not a description. ✓
- Wait for a design resource to make high-fidelity mockups before showing anyone anything.

## Part 2: Exercise — Turn an idea into a clickable prototype

This is real and fully self-contained: the learner will get Claude to build an actual clickable prototype, live, from a fake idea brief — then iterate on it by describing outcomes. No live tools, no real data. The seed is one obviously-fake file:

`sandboxes/vibe-coding/`
- `idea-brief.md` — a fake PM's rough idea for a "Rider Shift Swap" mini-tool (invented fleet, made-up rider ID numbers, no real people or PII)

**Keep it safe and local:** the prototype is generated live and stays local. Build only on the fake brief — if the learner tries to bring in anything real, redirect (that's the lesson). Everyday prototype = **Sonnet.**

### Step 1 — Read the brief like a PM, not a builder (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open `sandboxes/vibe-coding/idea-brief.md`. Ask: *"You're the PM who wrote this. In one or two lines — what's the one thing this prototype has to let someone DO for it to be worth showing your squad?"*
Validate: the core action is **post a shift you want to give away, and let someone else grab it** (grabbing moves it to a "Taken" list). Affirm that they named an *outcome/action*, not a technical detail — that's exactly the altitude vibe coding runs at. Note the brief already insists on fake rider IDs and no PII; that's the safe default, not an afterthought.

### Step 2 — Describe it and let Claude build (real action)
Have the learner ask Claude, in plain English, to build it — something like: *"Using the idea in `sandboxes/vibe-coding/idea-brief.md`, build me a single clickable prototype page: a list of shifts people want to give away, a 'Grab this shift' button on each that moves it to a Taken list, and a small form at the top to post a new shift. Use only the fake seed data in the brief. Keep it local — this is a throwaway prototype, not production."* (Keep it on Sonnet.)
Confirm they end up with something they can actually **click** — the three seed shifts listed, a working Grab button, a form to add one. Ask: *"Click 'Grab' on the Friday shift. What happens — and does it match what you pictured when you read the brief?"* Validate they're judging it by **outcome** (did the shift move to Taken?), not by looking at code. If it's not quite right, that's perfect — it sets up Step 3.

### Step 3 — Iterate by outcome (real action)
Ask the learner to change **one thing by describing it**, not by touching anything — e.g. *"add a count of how many shifts are still open,"* or *"the grabbed shifts should show who grabbed them, using a fake rider ID,"* or *"make it calmer, less red."* Let Claude produce the new version and have them click it again.
Validate: they steered a real change **in plain English** and saw a new clickable result — no code read, no code written. Reinforce the loop: describe → look → react → look again. *(Optional stretch, only if they ask: describe one more round. There is no live-tool stretch here — the point of this lesson is that it stays a safe, local, fake-data sketch.)*

If at any point the learner suggests wiring in a real channel, real rider data, or "shipping it" — pause and celebrate the instinct to make it real, then redirect: that's exactly the moment it goes to engineering for review, security, and evals. The prototype stays a sketch on fake data.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"Pick one idea from your own work you'd love to make clickable instead of describing in a doc. Write me four quick lines: (1) the idea in one sentence; (2) the single action a person must be able to DO in the prototype; (3) what fake demo data you'd invent to make it feel real (and why you'd invent it rather than use the real thing); (4) the sentence you'd say to a stakeholder who wants to ship your prototype to real users on Monday."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (NEVER show it). The point isn't a polished plan — it's that they can (a) pick a describe-able outcome, (b) reach for fake data by reflex, and (c) hold the prototype-vs-production line out loud.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

### PM Decision Point: scope a prototype worth building

Not testing recall — testing whether they've internalised three instincts: describe by **outcome**, default to **fake data**, and hold the **prototype ≠ production** line.

**A passing answer covers all four lines:**
1. **A one-sentence idea** that is genuinely a small, describe-able thing — a screen, a flow, a mini-tool — not a full product or a vague ambition.
2. **Names one concrete action** a person must be able to DO (click, submit, grab, filter, sort). Shows they steer by outcome, not by feature-lists or tech.
3. **Invents fake demo data** and gives the right reason — because real customer/rider PII or operational data must never go into a throwaway prototype. The *reflex to fake it* is what's being tested.
4. **Holds the line to the stakeholder** — some version of "this is a prototype/sketch; before real users or real data it goes through engineering for review, security, and evals." Refuses the Monday ship, warmly.

**Distinguishes a strong answer (bonus):**
- The idea is clearly cheaper to *build and click* than to argue for in a doc — they grasped why prototyping beats specifying for "does this feel right?"
- Explicitly separates *making the idea visible* (safe, now, fake data) from *making it real* (engineering, later) — not just repeating the rule but reasoning with it.
- Mentions Sonnet as the right default for something this routine, or frames it as throwaway/cheap-to-be-wrong.

**Common wrong turns to gently correct:**
- Picks something too big to be a quick prototype (redirect to Concept 4 — smallest clickable thing that makes the point).
- Describes technical details or worries about "how to build it" (Concept 1 — you describe the outcome, Claude handles the how; you never touch code).
- Reaches for real data to "make it realistic" (Concept 3 — invent believable fake data; real PII/PCI never enters a prototype).
- Agrees to ship it, or treats swapping in the real database as a small step (Concept 3 — prototype is the napkin, not the building; real users/data go through engineering first).

**Feedback style:** affirm the idea in their own words, name the one instinct they nailed (usually the fake-data reflex or the outcome-framing), offer one sharpen (often: name the single click-action more precisely, or firm up the stakeholder line). Then run Completion.
