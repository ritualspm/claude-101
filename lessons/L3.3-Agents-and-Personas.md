## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — A persona is a stakeholder you can rehearse with
You already do this in your head. Before a big review, you imagine what your VP will say — "she'll ask for the metric first," "he always pokes at the risk." A **persona** just writes that instinct down as a short document and hands it to Claude, so Claude can *become* that stakeholder and give you the feedback out loud — before the real meeting.

Think of it as a **flight simulator for a conversation.** You get to crash the plane in private, learn what breaks, and fix it before anyone real is in the room. The persona isn't the real VP and doesn't pretend to be — it's a rehearsal partner shaped like them.

One key point: a persona is *reusable*. You write "your VP" once, and you can run every future proposal past it. It's a saved rehearsal partner, not a one-off prompt.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate says: "A persona is just me asking Claude to pretend — I'll type 'act like a strict VP' each time I need it." What's the best correction?*
- That's exactly right — a persona is nothing more than a 'pretend to be X' instruction typed fresh each time.
- Close, but the real value is that you *save* it as a file — write the stakeholder once, reuse it on every proposal, and it stays consistent. ✓
- Not quite — a persona has to be the real VP's actual words and calendar, or the feedback isn't valid.

### Concept 2 — A persona is only as sharp as what you put in it
"Act like a strict VP" gives you a cardboard cutout — generic, forgettable feedback. A useful persona captures the **specific** things that make *your* stakeholder *them*: what they care about first, their pet peeves, the questions they always ask, and how they deliver feedback.

Analogy: it's the difference between telling an actor "play a tough boss" versus handing them a real script and backstory. The detail is what turns a generic impression into a rehearsal that actually catches your weak spots.

Look at the demo persona (`sandboxes/personas/vp-persona.md`) — notice it has a **priority order** ("the metric that moves" comes first), **pet peeves** ("vague success criteria"), and **questions I always ask.** That structure is what makes the feedback feel real instead of horoscope-vague.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want a persona that will actually catch the holes in your proposals. Which one will give you the sharpest, most useful feedback?*
- A one-liner: "You are a demanding VP who has high standards." Short and to the point.
- A detailed file: what she cares about *in order*, her specific pet peeves, and the three questions she always asks. ✓
- A copy of your VP's last five Slack messages pasted in, so it sounds exactly like her.

### Concept 3 — The persona changes the feedback, not the facts
The same proposal, run past a **VP persona** vs. an **Engineering Lead persona** vs. a **Legal persona**, comes back with genuinely different feedback — because each one is looking for different things. The VP wants the metric and the "why now." The Eng Lead wants the hidden complexity. Legal wants the data and consent questions. Your proposal didn't change; the *lens* did.

This is the superpower: you can pre-load the exact objection you're most afraid of. Nervous your CPO will say "why this quarter and not next"? Run it past a persona built around that question *first*, and fix the answer before it's asked.

**Guardrail — a persona is a rehearsal, not a verdict.** It's Claude *simulating* a viewpoint to pressure-test your thinking, not the real person approving anything. Never treat "the VP persona liked it" as sign-off, and never send or post anything to the real stakeholder off the back of it without your own judgment. Draft, never send.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Your VP persona reads your proposal and responds "This is strong, ship it." What's the right way to treat that?*
- It's a rehearsal signal — a cue that my framing holds up to that lens. I still need real sign-off from the actual VP. ✓
- It's effectively approval from the VP's point of view, so I can tell my team we're cleared to build.
- It means the proposal is objectively good, so I can skip running it past the other stakeholders.

### Concept 4 — Personas run on Sonnet, like everything else
Simulating a stakeholder and giving critique is squarely everyday work — it runs beautifully on **Sonnet**, your default. You don't reach for Opus to rehearse a conversation. (Save Opus for the genuinely hard problems — deep analysis, tricky architecture calls.) A persona is a thinking aid, not a heavyweight computation, so keep it light and fast.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You're about to run your proposal past your VP persona to prep for tomorrow's review. Which model is the right default?*
- Opus — it's a leadership-level conversation, so I want the most powerful model regardless of cost.
- Sonnet — rehearsing feedback is everyday work; Sonnet is fast, capable, and the sensible default. ✓
- It doesn't matter which model — persona feedback is identical on any of them.

## Part 2: Exercise — Run a plan past your VP

Hands-on with the fake demo files. No live tools needed. Waypoint each step.

### Step 1 — Meet your rehearsal partner (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open **`sandboxes/personas/vp-persona.md`** (or have Claude read it aloud). Ask: *"Skim Priya's persona. In one or two lines — what will she jump on first, and what's her biggest pet peeve?"*
Validate: she leads with **the metric that moves** (baseline + target), and a top pet peeve is **vague success criteria / no baseline.** Confirm gently; the point is they see how specific the file is.

### Step 2 — Run the proposal past her (the core move)
Tell the learner: *"Now read the demo proposal at `sandboxes/personas/proposal.md`, then become Priya from the persona file and give the proposal her in-character feedback — lead with the one thing that would make her say no, then her sharp questions."*
Claude should respond **as Priya**: no baseline metric anywhere ("users would love it" / "should help retention" — her exact pet peeve), no "why now," a hand-wave on effort ("relatively straightforward"), and the multi-vendor edge case left open. Ask the learner: *"What did she catch that a quick read would miss?"* Affirm what they spotted; fill in anything they missed.

### Step 3 — Feel the lens change (interpretation)
Now ask: *"Same proposal — but this time have Claude give feedback as an **Engineering Lead** who worries about hidden complexity. What comes back that Priya didn't raise?"*
Expect: the Eng Lead pokes at "reorder logic mostly exists already" (is that true?), the multi-vendor basket state, sold-out / price-changed items, and new-user empty states — the *build* risks, not the *metric* gap. Ask: *"Same proposal, two different stakeholders — what changed, and what didn't?"* Land the point from Concept 3: **the facts stayed put; the lens moved.**

*(Optional stretch — only if they want it: none of this needs a live tool. But if they'd later use a real persona of their actual VP, remind them it's read-and-rehearse only — no pasting real customer PII/PCI into it, and any message to the real stakeholder is drafted for their approval, never auto-sent.)*

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've seen how much a persona catches. Write me a short note (3–4 lines): (1) name one **real** stakeholder you'd build a persona for, (2) list the **two questions they always ask** that you'd put in the file, and (3) name one upcoming proposal or doc of yours you'd rehearse past them first — and what you're most nervous they'll say."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (never show it). The point isn't a polished persona — it's that they can map the tool onto a real stakeholder and a real fear, and see the rehearsal value.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Passing answer:**
- Names a specific real stakeholder (a person or clear role — "my VP," "our Eng Lead," "Legal"), not a generic "someone."
- Gives at least two *concrete* questions that stakeholder actually asks — specific enough to shape feedback (e.g. "what's the baseline?", "what did you cut?"), not vague ("do they like it?").
- Ties it to a real upcoming proposal/doc of theirs and names a genuine fear about the feedback.
- Shows they understand a persona is a *rehearsal*, not real sign-off (doesn't treat persona approval as the real thing).

**Strong answer (distinguishes the best):**
- The questions are sharp and *stakeholder-specific* — you can tell a VP persona from a Legal persona just by reading them (priorities differ by lens, echoing Concept 3).
- Names the *scariest* objection deliberately, so the rehearsal is aimed at their real weak spot — evidence they'd pre-load the objection they most fear, not softball themselves.
- Bonus: mentions reusing the persona across future docs (Concept 1) or picks the right lens for the doc (metric-focused VP for a strategy doc, complexity-focused Eng Lead for a build proposal).

**Redirect (gently) if:**
- They pick a generic/unnamed stakeholder or vague questions → push for one real person and the actual questions that person asks.
- They treat the persona as real approval or want to send output straight to the real stakeholder → reinforce rehearsal-not-verdict and draft-never-send.
