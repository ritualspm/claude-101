# L1.6 — Prompting That Works (say what you actually want)

**Course:** Using Claude · **Level 1 — Get Oriented** | ~22 min
**Part times:** Concepts ~11 min | Exercise ~7 min | Decision Point ~4 min
**Previous lesson:** L1.5 — Everyday Drafting (messages, one-pagers, meeting prep).

**WHO THE LEARNER IS (load-bearing):** A product person, likely still new to Claude. Teach through analogy and real PM tasks — never show code, never show prompt "syntax" or magic keywords. The whole point of this lesson is that a good prompt is just *clear delegation to a smart colleague*, not a secret formula. Keep it warm and practical.

**HOW TO RUN THE CHECKS:** Each concept ends with a multiple-choice check — clickable cards (`AskUserQuestion`) if available, else A/B/C. Correct option marked ✓ (never shown). Shuffle option order with the random command; one question per turn; always explain the outcome before the next concept.

## Part 1: The Concepts

Frame it up front: **You already know how to do this.** Prompting well isn't a technical skill — it's the same thing you do when you hand work to a sharp new teammate. The people who get the least out of Claude are the ones typing one vague line and hoping. This lesson gives you four habits that turn "meh" into "exactly what I needed."

### Concept 1 — Say the format, the role, and the goal (the intern test)

Analogy: imagine a brilliant intern on day one. If you say *"write something about checkout,"* you'll get a wall of text pointed at the wrong audience. If you say *"You're prepping me for Monday's leadership review. Write a half-page update on the checkout drop-off — problem, what we know, what's next — skimmable in 60 seconds,"* you get something you can almost use as-is.

Three things do 80% of the work:

- **Role / context** — who Claude is being for you, and who the output is for ("this is going to my VP").
- **Format** — a half-page note? three bullets? a table? a Slack message? Say it.
- **Goal** — what "good" looks like and why you need it.

Claude can't read your mind. Everything obvious in your head is invisible to it unless you say it out loud.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate shows you their prompt: "summarize the user research." The summary comes back generic and too long. What's the single most useful fix?*
- Tell them to switch to Opus — a stronger model will infer what they meant.
- Add who it's for and the format they want — e.g. "5 bullets for my PM peers, focus on the top pain points." ✓
- Tell them to run it three more times and pick the best output.

### Concept 2 — Load the context first, then ask

Analogy: you wouldn't ask a colleague *"what should we do about the drop-off?"* before handing them the data. Same with Claude. A prompt lands far better when Claude is **looking at the actual material** first.

For a PM that usually means: point Claude at the **folder** of docs, the **Slack channel**, or the **file** — *then* ask your question. "Read these three research notes, then tell me the top three themes" beats "what are common research themes" every time, because the second one is answered from thin air.

This is also where a guardrail lives: when you load real context, keep it **read-only**, and **never paste customer PII or payment (PCI) data** into a prompt. Point Claude at anonymized or internal material, ask your question about *that*.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want Claude to find the top complaints in a folder of 12 real customer-interview notes. What's the right move?*
- Paste the juiciest quotes — names and phone numbers included — straight into the chat so it has the richest detail.
- Ask "what do customers usually complain about in grocery apps?" and let it answer from general knowledge.
- Point Claude at the (anonymized) folder, ask it to read them, *then* ask for the top complaints. ✓

### Concept 3 — Ask it to critique its own work

Analogy: a good colleague doesn't just hand you a first draft — they say *"here's where I'm not sure."* Claude will do that too, but usually only if you ask.

Adding a line like *"before you finalize, point out the weakest part of this and one thing you might have gotten wrong"* is one of the highest-leverage habits there is. It surfaces shaky assumptions, thin evidence, and places it's guessing — **before** those things reach your VP. It costs you one sentence and saves you the embarrassing follow-up question in the review.

(This pairs with L1.7 — Trust & Verification. Self-critique is the *first* safety net, not the last.)

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude gives you a confident-sounding recommendation for a strategy note going to leadership. You have five minutes. What's the smartest quick step?*
- Ship it — a confident, well-written answer is a reliable signal it's correct.
- Ask it to name the weakest part of its own argument and what it might have gotten wrong. ✓
- Rewrite the whole thing yourself from scratch to be safe.

### Concept 4 — Iterate, don't restart

Analogy: if a teammate's draft is 80% there, you don't fire them and re-brief a new hire — you say *"great, now make it punchier and cut the second section."* Same here. The mistake beginners make is throwing away a near-miss and retyping the whole prompt from zero.

Claude remembers the conversation. So **steer**: "shorter," "more direct," "lead with the recommendation," "drop the jargon," "now turn it into three bullets for Slack." Each nudge builds on the last. Three small corrections get you somewhere a single mega-prompt rarely does — and it's faster.

And remember: everyday iterating like this runs great on **Sonnet**. You don't need Opus to say "make it punchier."

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Claude's draft one-pager is close but too formal and 20% too long. What gets you to a great version fastest?*
- Clear the chat and write one long, perfect prompt that specifies everything at once.
- Reply in the same thread: "good base — make it less formal and cut it by a fifth, keep the recommendation up top." ✓
- Accept it as-is; asking for changes tends to make Claude's writing worse.

## Part 2: Exercise — Sharpen a weak prompt, live

You'll take a deliberately vague prompt, improve it, and watch the output quality jump. This runs entirely on the demo file — no real data, no live tools needed.

**The demo file:** `sandboxes/prompting/weak-prompt.md` — a fake "checkout drop-off" scenario for a made-up grocery brand (QuickMart). It contains the weak prompt as someone actually typed it, plus all the context that was stuck in their head but never written down.

### Step 1 — Read the weak prompt (open-ended, short)
Have the learner open `sandboxes/prompting/weak-prompt.md` (or read the top of it aloud). The prompt is: *"write something about the checkout drop-off thing for the review."*

Ask: **"In your own words — if you handed this exact line to a new teammate with no other context, what three things would they have to guess?"** Validate against the file: they'd guess the *format* (note? deck? Slack?), the *audience* (who's "the review"?), and the *goal/scope* (what does "something" even cover?). Affirm whatever they spot; nudge toward the three missing pieces from Concept 1.

### Step 2 — Sharpen it together (hands-on)
Now the real thing. Ask the learner to **rewrite the prompt** using the context that's listed lower in the demo file (audience = Monday leadership review; format = half-page skimmable note; goal = problem / what we know / what we don't / next step; tone = honest about gaps). They can type their improved prompt straight to Ritto.

A strong rewrite names role+audience, format, and goal — e.g. *"You're prepping me for Monday's leadership review with my VP and two peer PMs. Using the notes in this file, write a half-page, skimmable update on the checkout drop-off: the problem, what we know, what we don't know yet, and one recommended next step. Be honest about the gaps."*

Then have Ritto actually **produce both outputs** from the demo file — one from the weak line, one from their sharpened version — and show them side by side. Ask: **"What's different about the second one, and which would you actually be comfortable putting in front of your VP?"** Let them notice the difference in their own words.

### Step 3 — Add a self-critique pass (hands-on)
Ask the learner to take their sharpened prompt and **add one more sentence** asking Claude to critique its own draft ("...then flag the weakest claim and anything you might be assuming"). Have Ritto run it and point out what the critique surfaced (e.g. that the root cause isn't confirmed, or that the numbers are directional). Ask: **"Did the self-critique catch something you'd have wanted to know before Monday?"**

**Optional stretch (only if they want it):** if they have a real, non-sensitive folder of their own docs connected, they can repeat Step 2 pointed at that folder instead — read-only, no PII/PCI. Frame this as optional; the exercise is complete on the demo file alone.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've felt the before/after. Now bank the habit. Write me a short 'prompt recipe' you'll actually reuse — 3 to 4 lines — for a real recurring task in your world (your Monday update, a stakeholder Slack, a research summary, whatever). It should name the **role/audience**, the **format**, and **what good looks like**, and include your **self-critique line**. Then add one sentence: which of the four habits was most missing from how you prompt today?"*

Max 2 prompts. The point isn't a perfect template — it's that they can turn a real task into a clear delegation and can name their own weak spot.

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Concepts (Part 1):** Four checks, one tap each. Correct answers: C1 → "add who it's for and the format"; C2 → "point Claude at the anonymized folder, then ask"; C3 → "ask it to name the weakest part of its own argument"; C4 → "reply in the same thread: make it less formal and cut it." On a wrong pick, give a one-line why (the distractors map to real mistakes: reaching for a bigger model instead of clearer instructions; pasting PII; trusting confident tone; restarting instead of steering) before moving on.

**Exercise (Part 2):** Passing = the learner identifies at least two of the three missing pieces (format / audience / goal) in Step 1, AND produces a sharpened prompt in Step 2 that adds at least role-or-audience + format. Strong = names all three missing pieces, writes a rewrite that hits role+audience, format, and goal explicitly, and articulates a specific quality difference in the two outputs ("the second leads with the recommendation and fits on half a page"). Step 3 passes if they add a self-critique clause and can name one thing it surfaced. Watch the guardrail: if they suggest pasting real customer data or PII, gently redirect (anonymize / read-only) — that's a teaching moment, not a fail.

**Decision Point (Part 3):** Passing = a reusable recipe that names at least role/audience + format + a rough sense of "good," tied to a genuinely recurring task of theirs, plus a self-critique line. Strong = all three of role/audience, format, and an explicit "what good looks like" bar, a self-critique line in their own voice, AND honest self-diagnosis of which habit they most neglect (context-first and self-critique are the two people skip most — reward specificity over a generic "I should be clearer"). Redirect if the recipe would send/publish anything automatically (draft-never-send) or leans on Opus for routine drafting (Sonnet-by-default).
