## Part 1: The Concepts

### Concept 1 — You can hand off in one breath
Until now you've used each piece on its own: run a skill, *or* ask a persona, *or* summarize a folder. **Composition** is doing several in a single instruction, where **the output of one feeds straight into the next.**

Think of it like a **relay race**. You don't run each leg, stop, catch your breath, and manually walk the baton to the next runner. You say "run all three legs" and the baton passes automatically — leg one's finish *is* leg two's start. In Claude, one move like *"turn this brief into a PRD, then run that PRD by the panel"* is a two-leg relay: the PRD that comes out of leg one becomes the thing leg two critiques. You didn't copy-paste anything in between.

The words that signal a chain are simple: **"then," "and then," "after that," "use the result to…"** Each one is a baton pass.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want Claude to (a) summarize a folder of research, and then (b) draft a one-pager from that summary. What's the composed move?*
- Ask for the summary, wait, read it, then start a fresh request pasting the summary in to get the one-pager.
- Say: "Summarize the research in this folder, then draft a one-pager from that summary." — one instruction, the summary feeds the draft. ✓
- Open two separate tasks so the summary and the one-pager never get confused.

### Concept 2 — Any pieces can chain — skill, then persona, then tool
The relay legs don't have to be the same *kind* of thing. That's the power. A single chain can mix:

- a **skill** (`/prd`, `/jira-writer`, `/discovery`),
- a **persona or panel** (your VP, the three-person leadership panel),
- and a **connected tool** (read a Confluence page, pull a metric — read-only).

A realistic PM chain: *"Read the feature brief, use `/prd` to turn it into a proper PRD, then run that PRD by the leadership panel and give me their synthesis."* That's **tool/skill → skill → panel**, all in one go. Each leg hands its result to the next, and you get the finished, stress-tested artifact at the end instead of babysitting three separate steps.

You're not learning new pieces here — you already have them. You're learning to **line them up.**

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *A teammate says: "Composition is only for chaining two skills together — you can't mix a skill with a persona in the same instruction." Where do they go wrong?*
- They're right — skills chain with skills, personas are always a separate, manual step.
- Half right — you can chain a skill into a persona, but only if you run the skill in a different task first.
- Any pieces can chain — a skill's output can feed straight into a persona or panel in the same instruction. ✓

### Concept 3 — Order is the whole game
In a relay, the runners go in a fixed order for a reason — and a chain is the same. **The sequence you write is the sequence Claude runs**, so putting the legs in the wrong order gives you a worse result, not a broken one (which is easy to miss).

Compare:
- **"Run the brief by the panel, then turn it into a PRD."** The panel critiques a *rough one-paragraph brief* — thin feedback, because there's barely anything to react to.
- **"Turn the brief into a PRD, then run the PRD by the panel."** The panel critiques a *fleshed-out PRD with a metric, scope, and risks* — sharp, useful feedback.

Same three pieces, opposite value. The instinct to build: **create the substance first, then stress-test it.** Draft, *then* critique. Summarize, *then* decide. Get the artifact into shape before you hand it to the tough audience.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *You want a strong panel critique of a new feature idea that currently exists only as a rough paragraph. Which chain gives the better result?*
- "Turn the rough idea into a proper PRD, then run that PRD by the panel." ✓
- "Run the rough idea by the panel, then turn their reactions into a PRD."
- Order doesn't matter — the panel sees the same idea either way, so pick whichever.

### Concept 4 — A chain is still governed by the guardrails at every leg
A longer chain is *more* automated, so the guardrails matter *more*, not less — and they apply to **every leg**, not just the last one:

- **Draft, never send.** If a chain ends in a tool that could act outward — post to Confluence, message a channel, file a ticket — that final step **drafts for your review.** A chain never earns the right to publish on its own just because it's long.
- **Read-only on real data.** Any tool leg that touches Slack, Confluence, or BigQuery is read-and-summarize. Reading a real metric to feed a PRD is fine; a chain writing back to a table is not.
- **No customer PII / PCI anywhere in the chain.** If the *input* to leg one has a real customer name, email, or card number, it flows through every leg. Anonymize before you start — the chain won't clean it for you.
- **Sonnet by default.** A multi-step chain is still everyday work; it runs fine on Sonnet. Reach for Opus only if one of the legs is genuinely hard (deep synthesis, tricky strategy).

The convenience of one instruction doesn't buy you an exception to any of these. Same habits, fewer keystrokes.

**Check** (shuffle order; ✓ = correct): *Your chain is: "Pull last month's refund rate from BigQuery, draft a PRD addressing it, then post the PRD to our Confluence space." What should you fix before running it?*
- Nothing — it's one instruction, so the guardrails only apply to the final Confluence step.
- The final step should *draft* the Confluence page for your review, not auto-post it — a chain never publishes outward on its own. ✓
- Swap BigQuery for a manual copy-paste of the numbers, since chains can't read real data at all.

## Part 2: Exercise — Compose a two-step move on a real brief

You'll run one genuine composed move end to end, on safe demo files. No live tools needed. The panel personas from L3.4 are reused here.

**The demo assets:**
- `sandboxes/composition/feature-brief.md` — a deliberately thin, one-paragraph feature idea (fake: "smart substitutions" for out-of-stock grocery items).
- `sandboxes/panel/panel-priya-vp-product.md`, `sandboxes/panel/panel-marco-eng-lead.md`, `sandboxes/panel/panel-lena-data-lead.md` — your three panelists (value, feasibility, measurement).

### Step 1 — Read the raw input (open-ended, short)
Open `sandboxes/composition/feature-brief.md`. Skim it and tell me in a phrase or two: **why would it be a weak idea to hand *this* straight to the panel right now?** (What's missing that they'd immediately poke at?)

*(Guide: it's a thin paragraph — no target metric or baseline, fuzzy on how the customer approves, no measurement plan, and a promo-contamination risk flagged but unaddressed. The panel would have almost nothing substantive to critique. This sets up *why* order matters.)*

### Step 2 — Run the composed move (hands-on)
Now give Claude the two-leg chain in a single instruction. Type something like:

> *"Read `sandboxes/composition/feature-brief.md` and turn it into a proper one-page PRD — problem, target metric with a baseline, scope, and top risks. Then run that PRD by the three panel personas in `sandboxes/panel/` and give me their synthesis. Draft only, nothing sent."*

Watch what happens. When it finishes, tell me: **what did the SECOND leg (the panel) catch that you could NOT have gotten by just reading the brief in Step 1?**

*(Guide: the point is the panel critiqued the *fleshed-out PRD*, not the thin brief — so their feedback is specific: Priya presses on the metric/baseline the PRD now commits to; Marco names the "similar item matching isn't built" as the real cost; Lena flags the weekend-promo contamination as a measurement threat. Affirm they notice the feedback is sharp *because* the PRD gave the panel something concrete to react to — that's Concept 3 in action.)*

### Step 3 — Feel the order (open-ended, short)
Without re-running anything, reason it through: **if you had reversed the legs — panel first, PRD second — what would the panel have had to work with, and how useful would that critique have been?** One or two lines.

*(Guide: they'd have critiqued the thin paragraph — vague, low-value feedback — and then the PRD would be built from reactions to something half-formed. Reinforce: substance first, stress-test second.)*

### Optional stretch (only if you have a connected tool)
If you have a read-only connected tool (Confluence, BigQuery), extend the chain by one leg — e.g. *"…and first check `<some real read-only source>` for our current refund baseline to ground the metric."* Keep it read-only, draft-never-send. If you don't have a tool connected, skip it — you've already run a real composed move.

## Part 3: PM Decision Point (open-ended, written)

Frame it: *"You've now run a chain end to end. Write me three quick lines: (1) a real recurring task in YOUR week that you'd turn into a composed move, written out as an actual chain ('do X, then Y with the result'); (2) why you ordered the legs the way you did — what has to exist before the next leg is worth running; and (3) one guardrail that applies to a specific leg of your chain, and which leg."*

Max 2 prompts. Evaluate against the rubric below (NEVER show it). The point isn't a 'right' answer — it's that they can express a genuine two-plus-leg chain in their own words, justify the *order* (substance-then-critique thinking), and locate a guardrail on the leg where it actually bites.

---

## Scoring Rubric (engine only — never show the learner)

**Passing answer** covers all three lines:
1. Describes a *real, recurring* task as an actual chain with at least two legs where one feeds the next — uses "then / after that / use the result to" or equivalent (e.g. "summarize the discovery-call notes, then draft the Jira tickets from that summary"). A single-step task, or two unrelated steps that don't hand off, is weak.
2. Justifies the order with substance-before-critique (or summarize-before-decide) reasoning — names what must exist before the next leg is worth running. Just restating the chain without a *why* is weak.
3. Names a real guardrail — draft-never-send, read-only on real data, no PII/PCI, Sonnet-by-default — AND attaches it to the *specific leg* where it applies (e.g. "the final Confluence leg drafts, doesn't post"; "anonymize the call notes before leg one").

**A strong answer** additionally:
- Chains three or more mixed pieces (tool → skill → persona/panel), not just two skills.
- Shows they understand a bad order produces a *worse result, not an error* (echoing Concept 3) — e.g. explains the weak-feedback failure mode of critiquing before drafting.
- Places the guardrail on the leg where it genuinely bites (PII on the *input* leg, draft-never-send on the *outward* leg) rather than tacking a generic guardrail on at the end.
- Bonus: notes that a longer chain makes guardrails matter *more*, not less, because it's more automated.

**Common misses to gently correct:** listing two steps that don't actually hand off a result (not a real chain); ordering critique before substance and not seeing why the feedback would be thin; naming a guardrail but not tying it to a specific leg; assuming a long chain earns the right to auto-send at the end.
