# Task Inbox — a normal Tuesday (demo asset — FAKE, for training only)

Four jobs landed in your lap today. They look similar on the surface — "ask Claude
to do a thing" — but they are NOT the same size of problem. The Model & Effort
lesson uses these to practice matching the tool to the job.

Nothing here is real. There is no real QuickMart, no real numbers, no real people.

---

## Task A — Reword a Slack message

Your teammate drafted a two-line message to the ops channel and asks:
> "can you make this sound less blunt? don't change the meaning"

The draft:
> "The dashboard is broken again. Someone needs to fix it before the standup."

That's it. No files, no context, no judgment call. Just a quick reword.

---

## Task B — The Monday weekly summary

Every Monday you write a short "what moved last week" update for your squad.
The raw material already exists: ~8 short Slack threads and two meeting-note docs,
all sitting in a folder Claude can read. The job is to read them and produce a
tidy, skimmable summary — bullet points, no drama, the same shape every week.

This is bread-and-butter work. You do it every week. It has to be reliable and
cheap, not brilliant.

---

## Task C — A strategy one-pager for your VP

You're proposing that QuickMart (a made-up grocery brand) shift its
grocery search from vendor-first to item-first. This one-pager goes to your VP
and will get picked apart in a leadership review. It needs a sharp problem
statement, an honest read of the trade-offs, and a recommendation that survives
tough questions. The writing quality and the reasoning both matter — this is the
kind of doc that changes what a team spends the next quarter on.

It is not technically hard. It is *judgment* hard. The nuance is the whole point.

---

## Task D — The gnarly funnel analysis

Checkout conversion dropped and nobody knows why. You have three messy data
pulls (obviously fake, for the demo) that don't line up cleanly: one is by day,
one is by device, one is by payment method, and the date ranges only half-overlap.
You need Claude to reconcile them, reason about what could plausibly explain the
drop, rule out the boring explanations, and lay out the two or three hypotheses
worth actually investigating.

This is genuinely hard reasoning across conflicting, incomplete data. There isn't
a clean answer sitting in one file waiting to be summarized — it has to be worked
out.

---

## Reminder for the exercise

For each task you'll pick a **model** (Haiku / Sonnet / Opus) and an **effort
level** (low / medium / high), and say *why* in one line. Lean on the
model & effort table in the lesson. There isn't a single "correct" grid — but
some picks are clearly wasteful and some are clearly under-powered.
